### Corrected entries, and what was corrected

**6 July, Francis Bacon, Essays, Of Adversity.** The edition. The line now follows Mary
Augusta Scott's edition of 1908, page 23, which capitalises Prosperity and Adversity
and prints the sentence whole. The 1625 spelling is Vertue of Prosperitie and Heroicall
Vertue, and other modern texts run the clause on from But to speak in a mean, so the
edition has to be named for the wording to mean anything.

**9 July, Julian of Norwich, Revelations of Divine Love.** The credit. Warrack's title
page calls the book a version from the manuscript in the British Museum, edited by
Grace Warrack, Methuen, 1901, so the source reads ed. and not trans. Her introduction
prints a variant with distressed; chapter LXVIII, which is the locator, reads
afflicted, and that is the reading on the card.

**10 July, Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice.** The preposition. The 1813 first edition
reads my courage always rises with every attempt to intimidate me. The circulating form
reads at every attempt. Volume II, chapter VIII of that edition is chapter 31 in modern
numbering, and Elizabeth says it to Darcy at the pianoforte at Rosings.

**17 July, Lu Xun, My Old Home.** The extent and the capital. The Yangs print the clause
as I thought: hope cannot be said to exist, with hope in lower case. The proposal
dropped I thought and capitalised Hope, which makes a sentence no edition prints. The
words are restored, and the volume is Selected Stories of Lu Hsun, Foreign Languages
Press, Peking, 1960.

**19 July, Dag Hammarskjöld, Markings.** The date. The entry stands in the opening block
of Markings, the entries headed 1925 to 1930, and not in 1958. Hammarskjöld was a young
man when he wrote it, which changes how the line reads. The English is Sjöberg and
Auden's of 1964 and is unchanged.

**21 July, Emily Brontë, No Coward Soul Is Mine.** The lineation. The carry-in came
through as prose. Rule seven says verse keeps its line breaks in the lineation of the
edition named, with the poem's own capitals at the line head, so the quatrain is
restored. Not a word is changed; only the four line heads recover their capitals.

**25 July, Antonio Gramsci, Letters from Prison.** The wording and the provenance. The
English that circulates is Raymond Rosenthal's, not Lynne Lawner's, and the card now
carries Lawner's own rendering of the letter to Carlo of 19 December 1929. The formula
itself is Romain Rolland's, which Gramsci took up in Ordine Nuovo in 1920 and credited
to him, and the note says so rather than printing it as his own maxim.

**26 July, Anne Frank, The Diary of a Young Girl.** The sentence boundary and the
translation. The famous line is not a sentence in Mooyaart-Doubleday's English but a
clause inside one, and the proposal capitalised it as though it stood alone. The comma
after everything and the reading truly good at heart belong to Susan Massotty's
translation of 1995, not to the 1952 text the card names.

**28 July, Antoine de Saint-Exupéry, Wind, Sand and Stars.** The chapter and the
speaker. The sentence is in chapter 2, The Men, not in Prisoner of the Sand.
Saint-Exupéry prints it in quotation marks as the words of the pilot Guillaumet, who
spoke them at Mendoza after walking out of the Andes, so Guillaumet stands in the
source. Galantière's wording of 1939 is exact.

**30 July, Hannah Arendt, Between Past and Future.** The section. It can be pinned after
all: the sentence closes section II of What Is Freedom? on page 156 of the Viking
edition of 1961, where it follows the claim that courage frees people from their worry
about life for the freedom of the world.

### Misattributions and false trails, found and kept out

These are traps the month walked up to and stepped around. They are recorded so nobody
proposes the bad form again.

* **Audre Lorde, Your silence will not protect you.** The sentence is genuine and
  correctly transcribed. Two things killed it. The source named Sister Outsider,
  Crossing Press, 1984, and nobody opened that setting: the document actually read is
  the version in The Cancer Journals, spinsters press, 1980. And the piece is a speech,
  delivered at a Modern Language Association panel on 28 December 1977, which its own
  headnote says in its first four words. The brief puts speeches and oratory out of
  scope on the Makers' ground, and the closed vocabulary has no tag for a delivered
  paper. If the owner rules that a conference paper gathered in a prose collection is an
  essay, the line can return on the 1980 text with the delivery disclosed in a note.
* **Herman Melville, the opening of Moby-Dick.** The citation is sound and the wording
  is verbatim for 1851. It was rejected because the proposed ellipsis cut about sixty
  words and kept twenty-five, and what it cut was the reason for the mood. A quotation
  that is publishable only because an ellipsis hides the passage's own crisis is a
  paraphrase wearing quotation marks, and the card would send the reader to the very
  paragraph it conceals. It is also a departure line, not a holding line, which is the
  wrong shape for July.
* **Dylan Thomas, Do not go gentle into that good night.** Line 1 of the villanelle,
  first printed in Botteghe Oscure in 1951 and collected by Dent in 1952, so the
  citation holds. It is out on publishability: the poem is an address to a dying father,
  and because it is titled by its own first line the source hands the reader the whole
  deathbed at six in the morning.
* **Elizabeth Cary, and the ghost of Lady Carew.** The four lines beginning The fairest
  action of our human life circulate in anthologies as Revenge of Injuries by Elizabeth,
  Lady Carew, wife of Sir Nicholas Carew. They are the Chorus that closes Act 4 of The
  Tragedy of Mariam, by Elizabeth Cary, in the 1613 quarto. The anthology text also
  drops the comma at the end of line 1 and turns the colon in line 2 into a semicolon.
  Held for a later month in the quarto's pointing, under the right name.
* **Antonio Gramsci, and Romain Rolland.** Pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the
  will is Rolland's formula. The apparatus of both English editions of the prison
  letters footnotes the sentence to him, and Rosengarten traced Rolland's own use of it
  in L'Humanité on 19 March 1920, weeks before Gramsci first reached for it. A card that
  prints it as Gramsci's own maxim with an empty note is a misattribution by silence.
* **Emily Dickinson, Aspiration.** The title is Mabel Loomis Todd's, not Dickinson's.
  The 1896 text also smooths her: the second stanza reads a daily thing where the
  manuscript text printed since Johnson reads a normal thing, and line 2 reads called to
  rise where the manuscripts read asked to rise. Held, cited by first line and by the
  standard numbers Fr1197 and J1176, with the editorial text disclosed.
* **Baruch Spinoza, and the Elwes year.** 1883 is Volume I of The Chief Works, the two
  political treatises. The Ethics is Volume II, and the copy whose title page and
  closing page were read is the revised edition of George Bell and Sons, 1891. A card
  citing 1883 would print a confident wrong date for the very volume it quotes.
* **Friedrich Hölderlin, and the Hamburger year.** The English belongs to Michael
  Hamburger and to nobody else, but Hamburger translated Hölderlin repeatedly across
  1961, 1966, 1980, 1986, 1994, 1998 and 2004, and the only edition-anchored citation
  anyone could reach was the 2004 Anvil text. A search inside the 1966 Michigan scan
  returned the passage itself, so 1966 stands.
* **Lillian Hellman, Watch on the Rhine.** The short form, every town, every village,
  every mud hut, is how the 1943 film renders the speech and how it travels online. The
  printed play carries the longer form with the three and joined clauses.
* **Mary Astell, on born slaves.** The widespread variant asking why are all Women born
  Slaves is not her wording, and the book is the 1706 third edition titled Reflections
  upon Marriage, without Some, whose added Preface carries the line.
* **Bertrand Russell, on conquering fear.** Transcriptions of the American printing read
  endeavor. The George Allen and Unwin text of Unpopular Essays, chapter 7, reads
  endeavour, and the sentence sits in the passage on fear, not at the close of the
  essay.
* **Blaise Pascal, the thinking reed.** The Trotter translation exists in two printings
  that point the sentence differently. The Harvard Classics setting of 1910 puts a comma
  after in nature; the later Everyman printing puts a semicolon. The year and the
  pointing have to match.
* **Louisa May Alcott, the battering-ram.** Not a journal entry of 1850. Cheney prints it
  inside a letter written from Boston to her father on 29 November 1856, her
  twenty-fourth birthday, on page 89, so the tag is Correspondence and not Diary.
* **Charlotte Brontë, I am no bird.** The circulating form cuts the sentence short and
  closes it with a full stop no edition prints. The 1847 first edition runs on to which
  I now exert to leave you, with a semicolon before it.

### Survivors held for later months

Forty-four survivors are held. The four that were nearest to landing and lost on the
balance rather than on the citation:

* **Emily Dickinson, We never know how high we are.** Dickinson stands at two for the
  year and the last slot should be spent deliberately, not on a month where nine Poetry
  lines were already seated. Routed to September.
* **Langston Hughes, Mother to Son.** Bang on the theme, and out only because the Poetry
  lane was full and because the poem wants a month that can give it the whole stair.
  Routed to November.
* **Bertrand Russell, To conquer fear is the beginning of wisdom.** Sits so close to
  Kant's Have courage to use your own reason that one of them had to go, and Kant took
  the day. Routed to October.
* **Simone de Beauvoir, To will oneself free is also to will others free.** A freedom
  line before it is a courage line. Routed to November.

The rest are in the unused array of month.json, each with the month it fits best.
Three more Poetry survivors from earlier months are still travelling: Homer's Tell me
about a complicated man, Adrienne Rich's My heart is moved by all I cannot save, and
Li Bai's I will mount a long wind.

Three carry-ins are dead on the year and should not be proposed again in any month:
01-fict-13 and 03-poet-02, both Chekhov, who stands at three of three; and 03-poet-01,
Shakespeare, who also stands at three of three. The citations are sound; the people are
finished.

### The critic's read, and 2 rulings

The critic filed two problems and both were upheld.

**The count of women was wrong.** The report said 11 and the month holds 12:
Lee, Charlotte Bronte, Abigail Adams, Izumi Shikibu, Nin, Julian of Norwich,
Austen, Emily Bronte, Anne Frank, Angelou, Akhmatova and Arendt. The floor of
8 was met either way. The figure is corrected here and in the report block.

**Day 15 put the speaker after the edition.** The house order is the speaker in
parentheses directly after the locator. Ruled: the parenthesis comes out
altogether rather than moving up. The poem is called Ulysses and the speaker is
Ulysses, so printing the name again tells a reader nothing.

Two things the editor raised are settled here as well. The carried Emily Bronte
line came through run together as prose and was restored to the lineation of
1850 with the verse convention: that is right, it is what rule 13 asks of every
verse line, and the wording was not touched. And the month's English originals
sit at exactly the ceiling of 16, so any later substitution in July has to come
from the non English side.
