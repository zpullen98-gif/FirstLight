### Corrected before landing

**3, Tennyson, Ring out the old, ring in the new.** The first verdict cited section CVI against the 1850 Moxon first edition. In that edition the poem is section CIV; it became CVI only after Tennyson inserted section LIX in 1851 and section XXXIX in 1869. The card now says CIV (Edward Moxon, 1850; CVI in the final text). The wording is the first edition's, point for point.

**6, Montgomery, to-morrow is a new day with no mistakes in it yet.** Proposed and first verified as tomorrow from Project Gutenberg 45, which names no source edition and modernises. Two scans of the 1908 L. C. Page first edition print to-morrow, hyphenated, twenty times and never otherwise. The card carries the hyphen and the edition. The refuter placed the line on page 246; the page numbers sit at the foot of the page in that edition, and the line is on page 247, the chapter's last page.

**8, Naidu, Rise, brothers, rise.** The proposal put a semicolon after the second rise and called the poem The Coromandel Fishers. The 1905 Heinemann edition prints a comma and titles it Coromandel Fishers, without the article. The line break after light is marked, under the convention below.

**9, Kierkegaard, life must be understood backwards.** The popular wording reads as the philosophers say. Dru's 1938 translation, in every reprint and every scholarly quotation of it, reads as philosophers say, with no article; Wikiquote's article is the 2002 Moran rendering. The Danish entry is Papirer IV A 164 (Journalen JJ:167), 1843.

**10, Chekhov, Welcome to the new life!** Garnett's wording and Trofimov as speaker hold. The first verdict called it the last exchange of Act IV, and it is not: Lopahin's Till the spring, Lyubov and Gaev alone, and Firs's closing speech all follow. The card now places it where it is, Trofimov answering Anya as the family leaves the house.

**20, Cavafy, As you set out for Ithaka, hope the voyage is a long one.** Two verifiers and the critic disagreed about which Keeley and Sherrard edition reads voyage and which reads road, and the editor ruled with the critic: the voyage wording is the 1975 Princeton Collected Poems, and the 1992 revised edition, the text the Poetry Foundation prints and credits, reads hope your road is a long one. The 1994 reading at Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis's funeral, which made the poem famous in English, used voyage, the older text. The card names the 1975 edition and the note says what the 1992 revision reads. Confidence medium, and honestly so: the Princeton pages themselves were not opened by anyone; the chronology rests on the 1992 credit at the Poetry Foundation and on the editor's own knowledge of the two texts. Anyone with the 1975 printing in hand should check line 2 and correct this file if it is wrong.

**26, Mansfield, Risk! Risk anything!** The proposal dated the entry 14 October 1922, her birthday, and read opinion in the singular. The 1927 Murry Journal, in both the Knopf and the Constable printings, heads the entry October 10 under The Final Step and reads opinions. The card carries the 1927 date and edition, and the note says the 1954 Definitive Edition redates the entry to the 14th, which no one could open.

**27, William James, Believe that life is worth living.** Proposed as the closing sentence of Is Life Worth Living?. It opens the final paragraph, after These, then, are my last words to you: Be not afraid of life, and the essay ends with the Henry IV and Crillon anecdote. James italicises is, which the card cannot show; the note says so.

**29, T. S. Eliot, We shall not cease from exploration.** The proposal ran the four lines together with a comma after exploration. Eliot has no comma and the lines are verse; the card marks the line breaks and follows the Faber text of 1942, collected in Four Quartets in 1944. Lines 26 to 29 of part V, counted from the first line of the section.

Two CORRECTED survivors were held rather than used. Sor Juana: trimmed to the one sentence confirmed in Peden's translation, Had Aristotle prepared victuals, he would have written more; the lead-in clause about trivial details was not checked against the edition and is left off.

### Found, and kept out, so nobody proposes them again

**And now we welcome the new year, full of things that have never been (Rilke).** The Calendar For Life card wording. It is a paraphrase; the Norton 1945 translation of the letter to Clara Rilke, Capri, 1 January 1907, reads And now let us believe in a long year that is given to us, new, untouched, full of things that have never been, and that is what ships, on day 2, from page 253 of the scan.

**The real voyage of discovery consists not in seeking new landscapes, but in having new eyes (Proust).** The circulating compression; no published translation reads so. Scott Moncrieff's The Captive has the real sentence, The only true voyage of discovery, the only fountain of Eternal Youth, would be not to visit strange lands but to possess other eyes, verified and held for April.

**The end of all our exploring will be to arrive where we started and know the place for the first time (Eliot), with a comma after exploration.** The comma is not Eliot's and the lines are verse; ships corrected on day 29.

**When you want something, all the universe conspires in helping you to achieve it (Coelho).** Real, but Clarke's translation begins the sentence with And, and Melchizedek speaks it; verified in that form and held for December.

**Tomorrow is a new day with no mistakes in it yet (Montgomery).** Real, but the unhyphenated tomorrow exists only in a modernised transcription; the 1908 edition has to-morrow.

**Life must be understood backwards, but it must be lived forwards, as the philosophers say (Kierkegaard).** Dru has no article. Anyone proposing the line with the should name the Moran 2002 translation, not Dru 1938.

**Hope the voyage is a long one (Cavafy, as 1992) and hope your road is a long one (Cavafy, as 1975).** Each is real; each belongs to the other edition. Voyage is 1975, road is 1992; the first verdict and the first draft of this file had them backwards.

**If it were customary to send little girls to school (Christine de Pizan, as Richards).** Brown-Grant 1999, not Richards 1982.

**One must imagine Sisyphus happy (Camus), The unexamined life (Plato), Beauty will save the world (Dostoevsky), To thine own self be true (Shakespeare).** The brief's own list flagged the last three as speaker traps; none was proposed. Camus was proposed, is real, and ships with O'Brien 1955 named.

Nothing on the brief's misattribution table was proposed by any lane.

### The critic's read, and four rulings

A critic read the finished month against the brief and filed four problems. The editor ruled on each before the month landed.

**Cavafy's edition (day 20).** Reversed: see the corrected entry above. The first verdict, its re-verification and the first draft of this file all had voyage as the 1992 wording; it is the 1975 wording.

**Verse lineation, one convention.** Three entries marked line breaks with a slash and four ran verse together as prose. The rule for this track from January on: a line of verse keeps its line breaks, marked with a slash and a space on each side, in the lineation of the edition named; a verse quotation is never run together as prose, and a poem's own capitals at the line head stand. Days 3, 8, 16 and 23 were re-lineated to their editions (Tennyson's four lines of section CIV; Naidu's two, the break the first draft dropped; Szymborska's four in Barańczak and Cavanagh; Dickinson's six as Todd printed them in 1896). Notes that only announced the marking were cut, since the convention is the track's.

**Garnett's dash (day 19).** Dostoevsky ships with the em dash Garnett printed. A quotation's own punctuation is exempt from the house rule against the dash; the source line and the note are not, and carry none.

**Two notes that read as context (days 10 and 29).** Both were corrections wearing the wrong clothes. Trofimov's line is often quoted as the play's last; it is not, and the note now says so in one sentence. Eliot's line travels with a comma after exploration that he never printed; the note now says that, and nothing else.
