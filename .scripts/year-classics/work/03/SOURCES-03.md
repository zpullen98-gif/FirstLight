### Corrected before landing

**1, Aristotle, we learn by doing.** The proposal dated Ross 1908 and printed lyreplayers as one word. Ross's Ethica Nicomachea first appeared in 1925 as volume IX of the Oxford Works of Aristotle, preface dated that year, and the printed text reads lyre-players. The refuter read the Oxford volume at 1103a32 to b2 and found the sentence word for word. The scanned reprint prints First Edition 1915 on its imprint page, which is the date of other parts bound in the same volume, not of Ross's Ethics. This is not the Durant line, We are what we repeatedly do, which is Durant's summary and not Aristotle.

**7, Mansfield, better far write twaddle.** Wording and entry as proposed. The page was corrected: the 1927 Knopf edition prints its folios at the foot, and the lines fall on page 246, not 245. They close the July 1922 Sierre entry, just before Murry's note on her return to London.

**11, Martineau, in full train.** Refuted and re-verified. Every word is hers, distaste to included, on p. 144 of the Boston Osgood edition of 1877. Her sentence runs on after a colon for thirty more words, and the card cuts at that colon and closes with a full stop; the note now says so, as February's Proust note does. The page is true only of the Boston two-volume edition: in the London Smith, Elder edition of the same year the sentence runs from p. 189 to p. 190 of volume 1, so the source names Boston and Osgood.

**15, Amiel, a tissue of habits.** Refuted and re-verified. The first verdict read Project Gutenberg 8545, which follows the American A. L. Burt reprint and reads habit is a living maxim, becomes flesh and instinct. Every Macmillan printing from 1885 to 1921 reads become, a participle, and the card ships become. The locator was re-anchored: the entry that follows the undated thoughts is 16 February 1851 in the first edition and 17 February from the second edition of 1888, so the source names only the entry before, 30 December 1850, and pages 7 to 8 of volume I. The philosophy lane proposed the last three sentences of the same paragraph; the whole paragraph landed and the tail is dead.

**21, Nietzsche, long obedience in the same direction.** Refuted and re-verified. The 1907 Macmillan edition of Zimmern prints a semicolon after direction; Project Gutenberg 4363, made from the later Levy reprint, prints a comma, and the proposal had copied it. The semicolon is restored. The 1907 edition sets obedience in italics, which the card cannot show, and the note says so. The line is long and carries the aside (to repeat it once more); it cannot be cut without minting, and it lands whole because it is the month's theme in Nietzsche's own words.

No line in March is a HEDGE.

Five source lines were tightened by the editor on the refuter's advice, with no change to any quotation and no note needed. **2, Trollope:** the year moved next to the title. **8, Johnson:** the quotation's pointing is that of the Cassell's National Library text (Project Gutenberg 652), so the title is spelled as that text spells it, Rasselas, Prince of Abyssinia; the 1759 first edition, titled Prince of Abissinia, points the passage differently (performed, not by strength, but perseverance: yonder palace, and no comma after three hours a day), every word the same. **9, Wang Yangming:** p. 30 of the Columbia 1963 edition added, read in place by the refuter. **10, Pope:** the wording with learn'd and the closing stop is that of the Works of 1717, so the source reads 1711; text of the Works, 1717. **18, Kenkō:** Porter's book is The Miscellany of a Japanese Priest, not Essays in Idleness, which is Sansom's and Keene's title; the source now gives the Japanese title and Porter's own.

One thing for a reader of day 12 to know: the ellipsis in Chekhov's letter to Nikolay is Chekhov's own suspension, printed by Garnett as four dots after will, not a cut. Nothing is omitted between the two sentences.

### Found, and kept out, so nobody proposes them again

**Man is most nearly himself when he achieves the seriousness of a child at play (Heraclitus).** The Calendar For Life card. It is not Heraclitus, whose fragment B52 is a different saying. It is a paraphrase of Nietzsche, Beyond Good and Evil 94, which in Zimmern reads The maturity of man, that means, to have reacquired the seriousness that one had as a child at play, with a dash after man. Verified and held for May.

**A thousand words will not leave so deep an impression as one deed (Ibsen).** An unsourced prose paraphrase. Garrett's 1894 verse Brand, Act II, reads A thousand words leave not the same / Deep print as does a single deed, and The Man speaks it; Herford's 1894 version reads A thousand speeches brand / Less deeply than one dint of deed. Garrett's couplet is verified and held for September.

**It is not the strong who survive, nor the wise, but the small hands that do the work (after Tolkien).** A paraphrase. Elrond's real sentence, p. 283 of the 1954 Fellowship, is verified and held for September.

**He who reads much and walks much, sees much and knows much (Cervantes).** Another rendering. Ormsby reads he who reads much and travels much sees and knows a great deal, and Don Quixote says it in wonder at a fraud. Held for May.

**I have raised a monument more lasting than bronze (Horace).** Another rendering. Bennett's 1914 Loeb reads I have finished a monument. Held for November.

**We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit (Aristotle).** Will Durant, The Story of Philosophy (1926), summarising Aristotle. No lane proposed it, and nobody should: the real sentence ships on day 1.

**Iron rusts from disuse; stagnant water loses its purity (Leonardo).** A later and freer rendering. Richter's 1883 text, no. 1177, reads Just as iron rusts unless it is used, and water putrifies or, in cold, turns to ice, with that spelling. Held for May, and it needs a note on the spelling before it lands.

**Knowing is not enough; we must apply. Willing is not enough; we must do (Goethe).** The popular wording. Bailey Saunders's 1893 maxim 324 reads It is not enough to know, we must also apply; it is not enough to will, we must also do. Held for May.

**Habit is a living maxim, becomes flesh and instinct (Amiel, as Project Gutenberg prints it).** A reprint's slip; Ward's Macmillan text reads become. See day 15.

**Long obedience in the same direction, there thereby results (Nietzsche, with a comma).** The e-text's reading; Zimmern 1907 prints a semicolon. See day 21.

**But he who dares not grasp the thorn (Anne Brontë).** She wrote he that. Shorter's 1920 Complete Poems, p. 145. Held for July.

**Builds up the structures of value, and at the crucial moments of choice (Murdoch).** Two variants in circulation, the first in the Stanford Encyclopedia. The 1970 book, p. 37, has neither the before structures nor the before crucial. Day 28 ships the book's reading; p. 36 belongs to the 2001 Routledge Classics, not the edition named.

**O snail / Climb Mount Fuji, / But slowly, slowly! (Issa).** That is Blyth's version and the Salinger epigraph. Hass's 1994 version reads Climb Mount Fuji, / O snail, / but slowly, slowly. The two must not be blended. Held for April, and the Ecco page should be opened first.

**Ian Fleming, one recipe for a best seller.** The essay is his (Books and Bookmen, May 1963) but the only text found is a composite online reprint that disclaims fidelity to the magazine. HEDGE, low confidence, thin fame. Held for June with that said; a later editor may fairly pass over it.

Nothing on the brief's misattribution table was proposed by any lane. The Hemingway lines verified this month are both from The Sun Also Rises and are neither of the false Hemingway lines on the table.
