### Corrected entries

Eleven of the thirty-one carry a note because the verifier or the re-verifier changed
something. No entry is hedged: every hedge proposed this month was refuted and then
rejected.

* Day 4, Charlotte Bronte, Jane Eyre. The speaker is now named after the locator.
  The book is presented as an autobiography edited by Currer Bell and Jane narrates
  in the first person, so the year's practice of naming first person narrators
  applies, as it does to Jim Burden and Jake Barnes. The wording is unchanged.
* Day 7, Aeschylus, Agamemnon. Morshead prints no full stop at line six: it ends in a
  comma and the sentence runs on, so the quotation now runs to line ten and closes on
  his own full stop. Line eight reads shows in the first edition of 1881 and signs in
  the revised second edition of 1889.
* Day 14, Anne Bradstreet, Contemplations. The ligature in Phoebus stands because the
  Ellis page prints it. Where in line four is not a slip: Ellis follows the 1678
  sheets and reads Where gilded o're, and modern editions emend it to Were. The long
  s in past is expanded. The 1678 sheets themselves were not opened.
* Day 17, Josef Pieper, Leisure the Basis of Culture. The pages given are those of the
  Pantheon Books edition of 1952 and not Faber, and the passage sits in chapter III of
  the title essay. The title in the Dru translation carries no comma. Gerald Malsbary
  renders the same sentence quite differently in 1998, so the translator must be named.
* Day 21, Elizabeth Bishop, letter to Anne Stevenson. The edition was corrected. The
  wording is confirmed and the passage was first made public as an excerpt in
  Elizabeth Bishop and Her Art in 1983. The Library of America volume of 2008 prints
  letters to Anne Stevenson, but that this one is among them could not be confirmed,
  so it is not cited.
* Day 22, Willa Cather, My Antonia. The locator was corrected. The sentence is the
  second to last of Book I, chapter II and not the closing words. The chapter ends
  with the sentence beginning When it comes to one.
* Day 23, Pablo Neruda, Keeping Quiet. The year was corrected. Alastair Reid's
  Extravagaria was first published by Jonathan Cape in 1972 and Farrar, Straus and
  Giroux issued the American bilingual edition in 1974. The Spanish original is A
  callarse in Estravagario, Losada, 1958. No printed copy was opened, so no page is
  given.
* Day 26, Etty Hillesum, An Interrupted Life. The translator credit was corrected. That
  edition prints its translator as Arno Pomerans and not Arnold J. Pomerans, though the
  two names belong to the same man. No page is given because no copy was opened. For a
  diary the dated entry is the locator.
* Day 27, Thich Nhat Hanh, The Miracle of Mindfulness. The locator was corrected. The
  sentence stands in chapter One, The Essential Discipline, on page 3, not in chapter
  Two. The earlier citation printed a section heading where the chapter title belongs.
  Beacon's contents page puts chapter Two at page 11, well after this passage.
* Day 28, Walt Whitman, When I Heard the Learn'd Astronomer. The edition claim was
  dropped. The text read carries no edition statement, numbers the two annexes as
  Books XXXIV and XXXV and omits A Backward Glance, so it is not the 1891 to 1892
  printing, and Book XX belongs to a later arrangement. The wording matches exactly.
* Day 29, Okakura Kakuzo, The Book of Tea. The punctuation was corrected. The 1906 text
  reads let us dream of evanescence, and linger in the beautiful foolishness of things,
  with a comma before and. Okakura wrote the book in English, so no translator is named.

### Kept out, and why, so nobody proposes them again

* Gustave Flaubert, be regular and orderly in your life like a bourgeois, so that you
  may be violent and original in your work. The French is not in doubt: Correspondance,
  volume IV, page 280, the letter to Madame Tennant headed Jour de Noel 1876. The
  English is the problem. No named published translation could be found that prints it.
  Exact phrase searches returned five different English sentences in secondary books and
  no volume of Flaubert's letters in English, not Steegmuller's Selected Letters of 1953,
  not the Harvard volumes of 1980 and 1982, not Rumbold of 1951. The circulating English
  also turns ordinaire into orderly, which loses the point the sentence is famous for,
  that the artist should be as ordinary as a bourgeois. Proposed as a hedge, refuted,
  rejected on re-verification. It is not shippable in English until a named translation
  is opened and quoted.
* Izumi Shikibu, watching the moon at dawn, solitary, mid-sky. The attribution is not in
  dispute but the words are. The only scan of The Ink Dark Moon is access restricted, and
  eighteen printed books that quote the poem disagree: eleven read at dawn and five read
  at midnight, the close is printed three ways, and some assign the poem to Women in
  Praise of the Sacred rather than to The Ink Dark Moon. No page exists anywhere and the
  lineation of the named edition is unknown, so rule 7 cannot be met either. Both the
  wording and the line breaks would have to be chosen rather than found. Proposed as a
  hedge, refuted, rejected on re-verification.
* Elizabeth Barrett Browning, earth's crammed with heaven. Real and hers, but already
  shipped: month 6 carries the same passage at greater length, running on through the
  rest sit round it and pluck blackberries. The 1857 page images also set a comma after
  takes off his shoes and not the full stop proposed. Rejected at the verdict stage.
* Simone Weil, attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity. Carried to October
  from January and February and blocked by the cap: Weil already stands three times in
  the year, in February, March and September. The line cannot land in any month. It
  should be retired from the carry list rather than carried again.
* Michel de Montaigne on travel, Essays, Book III, chapter 9. The refuter of the back
  shop line flagged the collision before it happened: two Montaigne lines were both
  pointed at October, which would have put him twice in the month and four times in the
  year. The back shop is the stronger line on this theme by a distance and it takes
  day 5, which brings Montaigne to three for the year. The travel line is now blocked by
  the cap and should be retired rather than carried again.

No line from the misattribution list was proposed this month.

### The critic's read, and 4 rulings

All four are upheld. Two were settled by opening the page.

**Day 18, Sappho, mixed two numbering systems.** The card cited fragment 104a,
which is the number modern editors use, while naming Wharton's Sappho and the
Symonds rendering of 1883. Wharton's own text was read at the fragment. He
prints it as 95, with the Greek, a literal prose version, and then the verse
renderings, of which the Symonds one is word for word what the card carries.
The card now cites 95, so the number and the edition named agree. Anyone
working from a modern edition should look for 104a.

**Day 28, Whitman, had lost its edition.** An earlier citation named a printing
nobody had opened, and dropping it left the card with a cluster name and
nothing else. The 1891 to 1892 McKay printing has now been read: its own
contents page puts When I Heard the Learn'd Astronomer at page 214, inside By
the Roadside, and the text on the page matches the card exactly, down to
wander'd and Look'd. The card names that printing and that page.

**Days 4 and 27 used straight apostrophes in their notes**, where every other
note in the month uses curly ones. Upheld, and swept across the whole month and
the held pile rather than fixed twice: the gate refuses a straight double quote
but says nothing about the apostrophe, so this is the kind of thing that
accumulates quietly.

One note for whoever builds November. The Calendar For Life curator was stopped
by a content filter again, as in April, so three lanes did the proposing. The
month still came in full. Fifty carried lines now face two months, so the
weakest have to be let go rather than carried forward a third time.
