### Corrected entries, and what was corrected

**1 June, Iris Murdoch, The Sovereignty of Good.** The page. The proposal cited p. 91
of the Routledge and Kegan Paul printing of 1970, which nobody opened; a first reading
moved it to p. 90; re-verification read the running head and settled it at p. 89 of the
Routledge edition of 2014, which is the edition the card now names.

**2 June, Miguel de Cervantes, Don Quixote.** The wording. Ormsby breaks Sancho's
speech with a narrative tag, so the address and the sentence about the windmills are
not one sentence on the page, and the proposal had joined them with a comma. The
sentence now ships alone, word for word in Ormsby, with a capital at the head. The
refuter confirmed the translator against two witnesses because Don Quixote has more
competing English texts than almost any book in the language.

**3 June, Robert Burns, To a Louse.** The wording. The proposal read Power and ithers.
The 1786 Kilmarnock text digitised by the National Library of Scotland and the 1787
Edinburgh edition both print Pow'r and others, so the familiar ithers is in neither
and the line ships as Burns published it.

**4 June, Sarah Moore Grimké, Letters on the Equality of the Sexes.** The printed
sentence in the Isaac Knapp text of 1838 opens with the word But. The quotation begins
at I, so one conjunction is dropped and nothing else is altered. The letter is dated
Newburyport, 7th mo. 17, 1837 and addressed to Mary S. Parker, and the card now says so.

**14 June, Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Aurora Leigh.** The punctuation. The page images
of the first edition of 1857 set a comma after takes off his shoes where the proposal
had a semicolon. The lines are Aurora's own narration in the Seventh Book, and the
quotation begins in the middle of a verse line.

**19 June, Henrik Ibsen, An Enemy of the People.** The locator. This is Dr. Stockmann's
last speech, not the closing words of the play: two short speeches follow, and Petra
has the final word. The wording is the Farquharson Sharp translation of 1911, which the
quotation sites usually render loosely as a bare aphorism. The dash inside the sentence
is the translation's own punctuation.

**21 June, Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, Response to Sor Filotea.** The title. Peden's
volume heads the work Response to the Most Illustrious Poetess Sor Filotea de la Cruz,
and the card prints the short form. These words also close a longer sentence, so the
opening capital is the card's, and the page rests on one secondary citation rather than
on an opened book.

**23 June, Nishida Kitarō, An Inquiry into the Good.** The extent. An earlier reading
cut the sentence at they are and closed it with a stop the book does not have. The
whole opening sentence now ships, as every witness prints it. A scholarly citation
places it at p. 3 of Abe and Ives, but the Yale page was never opened, so no page is
printed.

**24 June, Wisława Szymborska, Under One Small Star.** The edition and the year. The
line stands verbatim on p. 91 of View with a Grain of Sand, Harcourt, 1995, where the
Barańczak and Cavanagh rendering first appeared. Poems New and Collected of 1998 only
reprints it, so the translation year is 1995.

**25 June, Henry David Thoreau, Journal.** The editor. The Journal volume of 1906 names
Bradford Torrey alone, not Torrey with Francis H. Allen, who joined the edition later.
The volume and page, II and 373, are added.

### Hedged entries, and why

**9 June, Su Shi, Written on the Wall at West Forest Temple.** No printed copy of Burton
Watson's translation was opened. Two secondary witnesses give this wording exactly; a
third spells the mountain without its hyphen and misnames the translator, so it carries
less weight. The translator is therefore named as attributed, and the page number that
one site supplies for the Copper Canyon edition of 1994 is dropped. The poem is four
lines, so closing couplet locates the line in any printing.

### Misattributions and false trails, found and kept out

These are traps the month walked up to and stepped around. They are recorded so nobody
proposes the bad form again.

* **Virginia Woolf, on dining.** The Woolf Society of Great Britain lists
  One cannot EAT well, love well, sleep well, if one has not dined well as a
  misquotation. The genuine sentence has think well. One verb is the whole difference
  between a citation and a flagged misquote. The true form is held for a later month.
* **Mark Twain, Travel is fatal to prejudice.** The Calendar For Life bank and every
  quotation site print a serial comma after bigotry. The 1869 American Publishing
  Company setting, checked in two Internet Archive scans at p. 650, does not. The card
  form has no serial comma there.
* **Italo Calvino, on classics.** A search for definition 6 returns
  a book which has never exhausted all it has to say to its readers. That is Martin
  McLaughlin's rendering of 1999, not Patrick Creagh's of 1986. The card uses Creagh,
  read on the New York Review page itself.
* **John Keats, on books and wine.** The circulating form, which Calendar For Life
  carries at cflId 340, reorders the list and rewrites the clause: books, French wine,
  fruit, fine weather and a little music played out of doors. Colvin's 1891 text reads
  Books, fruit, French wine and fine weather and a little music out of doors, played by
  somebody I do not know. The circulating form is out.
* **Ralph Waldo Emerson, on truth and repose.** The corrupt rendering inverts the pair
  to a choice between repose and truth. The 1841 text has truth first.
* **Marianne Moore, Poetry.** The 1919 and 1924 printings read I too, dislike it with
  one comma and carry the sentence over a line break, so the proposed wording and
  locator did not belong to the same edition. Only Moore's own final three line text of
  1967 supports the card form.
* **Adrienne Rich, Prospective Immigrants Please Note.** Snapshots of a Daughter-in-Law
  was published by Harper and Row in 1963; Norton reissued it in 1967. Putting Norton
  on the first edition is the common error.
* **Omar Khayyám, quatrain XXVII.** FitzGerald's fifth edition reads great argument in
  lower case and the same door where in I went. The card is first edition text, so
  first edition in the source line is load bearing and not decorative.
* **Jorge Luis Borges, Funes.** To think is to forget a difference, to generalize, to
  abstract is Andrew Hurley's later rendering. The card that survived is James Irby's
  in Labyrinths. Two renderings of one sentence must never both ship.
* **Muriel Rukeyser, The Speed of Darkness.** The popular shortening to stories not
  atoms loses the poem's own lineation and comma. The card keeps both lines.
* **Hans Christian Andersen, The Emperor's New Clothes.** The drifted versions lose the
  word got, and some attach the town's later exclamation mark to the child's sentence.
  The child's line ends in a full stop four lines before the town cries out.
* **Henrik Ibsen, on standing alone.** The quotation sites carry a bare aphorism with
  no It is this, let me tell you. The card carries what Farquharson Sharp printed.
* **Robert Burns, To a Louse.** Power and ithers are both modern. See above.
* From the standing list in the brief, nothing was proposed: no Gandhi slogan, no
  Einstein on insanity, no Wilde be yourself, no Voltaire on defending what you say,
  no Burke on the triumph of evil, no Hemingway at a typewriter. The list did its work
  before the lanes wrote.

### The critic's read, and 0 rulings

The critic found nothing to file. The operator read the month and ruled on
nothing, having checked the line most likely to look like an error: 3 June,
Burns. Every reader knows it as to see oursels as ithers see us, and the card
prints others. The Kilmarnock text of 1786 was opened at the page through the
full text search of the Internet Archive, and it reads, in the long s of the
printing, O wad ſome Pow'r the giftie gie us, To ſee ourſels as others ſee us.
The familiar ithers belongs to later editors, not to Burns's own first
edition, and the card is right. Gutenberg's modern collected text, which
prints Power and ithers, is what a checker will find first and is not an
edition of 1786.

16 June holds the closing words of Ulysses, on Bloomsday, which is why that
carried line was routed to this month.
