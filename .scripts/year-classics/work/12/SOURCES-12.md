## December: 31 of 31

Theme: Renewal and Hope. Closing lines, and the promise the books keep: the last
sentence opens on a beginning.

### The count

Candidates proposed: 48. Three lanes of sixteen: fiction and essay, philosophy,
poetry and drama. No Calendar For Life lane ran this month.

Verdicts written: 46. Two fiction and essay candidates were never verified on their own,
because each duplicated a line the philosophy lane had already proposed: 12-fict-14 is
Havel on hope (verified as 12-phil-09) and 12-fict-15 is Camus's invincible summer
(verified as 12-phil-01).

Survivors from December's own lanes: 44. Two died, both on rule 2 (fame) after a
refutation and a rejecting re-verification, and both are recorded below. The
philosophy lane came through fifteen of sixteen, the poetry and drama lane sixteen of
sixteen, the fiction and essay lane thirteen of sixteen counting the two duplicates.

Carry-ins routed to December by earlier months: 95.

Survivors in all: 139 records.

Used: 31. Twenty-eight from December's own lanes and three from the carry list.

### Placement

Day 1 is Hiratsuka Raicho's In the beginning, woman was truly the sun, an opening
sentence for the opening day. Day 2 is the close of the Purgatorio, where Dante comes
back from the water regenerate like new trees: renewal stated rather than fitted.
Day 21, the winter solstice, takes Camus's invincible summer. Day 23 takes Lincoln's
letter to Fanny McCullough, which is dated 23 December 1862. Day 25 is Rimbaud's
dawn and its magnificent cities. Day 30 is Hardy's Darkling Thrush, written on the
last day of a century, and the month and the year close on day 31 with the closing
lines of Kushner's Perestroika: More Life. The Great Work Begins. Forms and centuries
alternate through the month; no two lines of one form stand on consecutive days.

### CORRECTED entries used

* Day 7, Seamus Heaney, The Cure at Troy. The printed play capitalises Don't after
  History says; the proposal had lowered it. The refutation then showed the lines open
  the third stanza of the Chorus speech Human beings suffer, not the second, and the
  re-verification confirmed it against the Noonday scan of 1991.
* Day 9, Oodgeroo Noonuccal, A Song of Hope. The 1964 Jacaranda first edition, printed
  under the name Kath Walker, reads a new bright day, not a bright new day, and carries
  commas the web texts drop. The refuter traced the variant to the earlier magazine text
  in Freedomways, Winter 1963, not to a later reprint as the verifier's note had it, so
  the note was rewritten. The source adds as Kath Walker so the edition can be found.
* Day 11, Ralph Waldo Emerson, Circles. The locator was corrected: in the James Munroe
  Essays of 1841 the sentence closes the opening paragraph, page 249, not the second
  paragraph. The 1841 book was titled Essays, not First Series.
* Day 14, Octavio Paz, Sunstone. Corrected by the editor on the refuter's evidence: the
  edition is now the New Directions bilingual Sunstone of 1991, whose text was seen,
  because the 1987 Collected Poems page was not opened. The poem ends on a colon, closed
  to a full stop. The six lines are both the close and the opening of the poem.
* Day 15, Etty Hillesum, An Interrupted Life. The locator is narrowed to the last
  diary entry, page 196 of the Pantheon 1983 selection. The verifier's note claimed the
  complete edition words the Dutch differently and that a line in capitals follows in
  the manuscript; the refuter found neither claim supported (Coetsier cites this English
  wording to both complete editions), so the note now says only what is known and gives
  the Dutch: Men zou een pleister op vele wonden willen zijn.
* Day 16, Alexandre Dumas, The Count of Monte Cristo. Refuted as first proposed: the
  wording was the Project Gutenberg text, which names no translator and differs from
  the Chapman and Hall printing of 1846 it was cited to. Re-verified against the 1846
  page images, vol. II p. 463, which read will deign and is contained where Gutenberg
  has shall deign and is summed up. It ships in the 1846 wording.

Two VERIFIED entries take the refuter's sharper citation without a change of wording:
Beethoven (day 3) now cites Anderson's letter number, 54, and Havel (day 22) names
Knopf. The Beethoven line runs on after lives with a dash in Anderson; the full stop
closes the excerpt, as Lockwood also prints it.

### Dead, and why

* 12-fict-13, Tove Jansson, Moominland Midwinter (Now I've got everything). The wording
  is genuine in Warburton, but it is neither the opening nor the closing line (about a
  fifth of the book follows it), not the sentence the book is known by, and no standard
  reference entry was found. The proposal's closing locator was wrong.
* 12-phil-07, Christine de Pizan, The Book of the City of Ladies, III.19.1 (My most
  honored ladies). Verbatim in Richards 1982, p. 254, but it opens the last chapter
  rather than closing the book (five sections follow), and no reference entry was
  found. The same ground on which April dropped Kalidasa.

### Wordings kept out, so nobody proposes them again

No line this month travelled under a false name. These are wrong wordings of real
lines:

* Dumas, Wait and hope: the Project Gutenberg wording (shall deign, summed up) is not
  the Chapman and Hall 1846 text. Cite the 1846 wording or a named edition read.
* Oodgeroo, A Song of Hope: bright new day is the Freedomways magazine text of 1963,
  not the book.
* Levertov, Beginners: fullness with two l's and capitalised line heads are anthology
  drift; Candles in Babylon (1982) prints fulness and lower case.
* Beethoven to Wegeler: take Fate by the throat, never overcome me and would I could
  have a thousand lives belong to older translations, not to Anderson.
* Hillesum: the claim that a capitalised line follows the last sentence of the diary is
  unconfirmed. Do not repeat it.

### The critic's read, and 1 ruling

The critic filed nothing, so the operator's own read is the ruling. What was
checked, and against which page:

* Ruling, day 12, Maeterlinck, The Blue Bird, the closing lines. Read in the
  1910 Musson printing in six acts (archive.org bluebirdfairypla00maet_0, its
  djvu text, the page before the printer's line). It prints "give him back to
  us? . . . We need him for our happiness, later on. . . ." The three points
  after the question are the translator's, so the card hides no cut, and the
  four points at the close keep the translator's own full stop, which is what
  the card prints. That is the practice 2 November already follows and says so;
