## The Rig Veda, days 65 to 96 (veda-03)

Sixty four candidates were proposed, a primary and a standby for each of the thirty two days, in six lanes; the lanes for days 72 to 76 and 85 to 88 were proposed a second time (L2r and L4r) after the first runs of L2 and L4 stopped short, and the work is read in Ralph Griffith’s translation. Sixty of the candidates named Sāyaṇa and four named Yāska, all four of them standbys (days 66, 70, 87 and 91). All thirty two primaries were verified: twenty four as VERIFIED at first judgement and eight (days 70, 83, 87, 88, 89, 92, 95 and 96) as CORRECTED. Each primary was then attacked by a refuter who reopened the packet source, on several days the pinned Wikisource revision itself. Four were refuted (days 70, 84, 87 and 96); each was judged a second time, CORRECTED, and lived. A critic then read the whole batch five times, and each reading sent entries through a round of revision, every revision attacked again by a fresh refuter who reopened the source: five revisions in the first round, of which three were cleared and two refuted; thirteen in the second, of which twelve were cleared and one refuted; one in the third, which was refuted; two in the fourth, both cleared; and two in the fifth, both cleared. Twenty three revisions were made in all, on fourteen days (65 to 70, 75, 83 to 88 and 96); nineteen were cleared and four refuted. No primary fell at any stage. Status gives thirty two living primaries, and all thirty two are used. The thirty two standbys were never verified, because no primary fell. The editor changed nothing in any entry: every entry is copied exactly as status.js gives its survivor, and none held a straight quote to be made curly. At the edit after the fourth round the batch differed from the first edit on days 84 and 88; at this edit, after the fifth round, it differs from that one on two days only, 75 and 84, where the fifth round’s revisions were cleared; the other thirty entries are the same, word for word. The candidate gate, which joins the batch to the other veda days already landed, passes with ninety four days, no error and one warning, that Sāyaṇa holds ninety one of the ninety four; in this batch Yāska now holds days 66 and 75 and otherwise stands only in unverified standbys, and the critic found no other key verse in the batch that the Nirukta treats, so the warning asks for nothing the editor may do.

The spread. Sāyaṇa is named on thirty days and Yāska on two (day 66, Nirukta 5.10, and day 75, Nirukta 5.7 to 5.8); no day names two commentators. No entry is hedged: every Sāyaṇa reading is taken from his own Sanskrit on Sanskrit Wikisource, in the pinned revision the packet names for each hymn, with Wilson’s translation (volumes 4 to 6 on archive.org) and on some days Griffith’s notes used only as a check that the gloss is his; the Yāska readings on days 66 and 75 are taken from Lakshman Sarup’s translation of the Nirukta, opened on archive.org, and on both days the same Nirukta words stand verbatim in Sāyaṇa’s commentary in the packet, marked as the Nirukta. For Book 8 the place is given by the app’s numbering, as the roster trap requires, and on days 87 to 92 the source line adds the standard number in parentheses (app 8.56 to 8.91 are standard 8.67 to 8.102). On day 86 the packet warns that Sāyaṇa counts fifteen verses in 8.48 to Griffith’s fourteen; the verifier found that the Sanskrit labelled 8.48.3 is the app’s verse, so the numbers agree at the key. Every sentence in the batch now gives the place in one form, “Sāyaṇa, on m.h.v”, or for the Yāska days “Yāska, Nirukta 5.10” and “Yāska, Nirukta 5.7 to 5.8”.

The CORRECTED entries at first judgement. Day 70: the proposal had Sāyaṇa take Visnu “as the self of all the gods”, which inverts sarvadevātmaka (having all the gods as his nature) and lends it a Vedāntic colour not in his words; it became “as comprising all the gods”, and “as his branches” became “as though his branches” to keep his iva. The Aitareya reference was left unnamed as “a Vedic text”, since the parenthesis may be the editor’s. Day 83: the proposal had the men “beginning” the Agnihotra at sunrise; his word is kurvanti, so it became “performing”. Day 87: the proposal placed the fishes caught in a net on 8.56.8, where they are not; the verifier moved them to Sāyaṇa’s preface to the hymn and cited both places (this clause was later removed, below). Day 88: the two who direct the five became pratiprasthātṛ and adhvaryu, as Sāyaṇa names them, rather than loosely “adhvaryu priests”; the one hot offering was given as his word gharma; and the context clause placed the verse after the horses and car of verse 6 (all later reworked by revision, below). Day 89: the proposal gave only one of Sāyaṇa’s glosses on the covering and drifted towards barrenness and clothing; the sentence now carries both of his alternatives, fruit for the fruitless or cloth brought forth, and the context clause was shortened to hold forty five words. Day 92: Sāyaṇa glosses manasā as manasā eva śraddadhānaḥ, keeping the mind and adding faith, so “reads the heart as faith” became “reads the heart as a mind of faith”. Day 95: the plain sense said the hymn “here turns to the straining of the juice”, but verses 2 and 3 already have Soma flowing purely, so it now “follows the juice as it is purified”; the reading, that the begetting is figurative (upacāra), was his and unchanged. Day 96: the plain sense restated the verse’s own content and misplaced the refrain, and the reading had Sāyaṇa read the verse through the Śāṭyāyanaka story, when he glosses the verse and cites the story as a parallel; the sentence then said he takes the givers as kings and cites the Śāṭyāyanaka.

The refutations and the second judgements. Day 70 was refuted on the plain sense: “a hymn calling the gods to the rite” is not what the text says, since in Griffith 7.40.1 the assembly gathers to begin the praise of the gods, and the summons came from Sāyaṇa’s own address to the gods in his gloss on 7.40.1, so the commentator’s frame had entered the plain sense. The second judgement changed the context clause only, to “a hymn where the assembly gathers to praise the gods and asks them for riches”. Day 84, VERIFIED at first, was refuted on the plain sense and on tone: every verse of 8.39 ends on the wish that all the others die away, and “wishing all the others away” softened a wish for their death against rule 6; and the text calls Agni the worshipper’s messenger and our envoy, not “the gods’ messenger”. The second judgement restored the refrain as the text has it, made Agni the worshipper’s messenger, changed “born of the senses’ objects” to “born of sense contact” (viṣayayoga) and “whichever god” to “whatever god is invoked”. Day 87 was refuted on attribution and form: the list of seers in the preface is not Sāyaṇa’s reading but the Anukramaṇī, which he quotes after atrānukramaṇikā, and the clause about fishes caught in “one among its possible seers” was hard to parse, with evidence covering only verse 8. The second judgement removed the preface clause entirely and kept only what his words on 8.56.8 carry: the fetter as a binding net, and Indra, controller of all, keeping the speakers clear of it; the source line was narrowed to 8.56.8 (standard 8.67.8). Day 96 was refuted because the context clause never named Soma, against the voice rule, and opened “Its last two verses” with no antecedent under a printed key verse, and because “uneasy at so much” softened the Brāhmaṇa’s garagirāv iva menāte. The second judgement made the clause “Closing a hymn to Soma, the singers turn to the gifts received”, and rendered the image as “feeling like swallowers of poison”, declining the refuter’s “dreading such wealth like poison” as adding a dread the words do not carry. In all four the key, the reference and the evidence were unchanged.

The first round of revision. The first critic raised six problems, and five entries went through a revision.

Day 66, cleared, and the one change of commentator. The critic found that Sāyaṇa’s gloss on 7.1.1, the fingers for the word Griffith renders deep thought, is quoted verbatim from the Nirukta, which he marks as Nirukta 5.10, so the reading is first Yāska’s. The reviser opened Sarup’s Nirukta on archive.org (the djvu text of nighantuniruktao00yask): the verse is quoted on page 78 and explained on page 79, where the dīdhitis are the fingers, so called because they are employed in actions, and the fire sticks are so named because fire rests in them or is produced from them by attrition. The day was given to Yāska with the plain sense kept (“that asks” trimmed to “asking”) and a source line naming Sarup. The refuter reopened the same text, found the passage between the ends of the ninth and tenth sections, and cleared it; no hedge is needed, since Yāska is on the roster and treats the verse. Day 84, refuted. The critic found the refrain’s “all the others” unexplained, so that a reader cannot tell who they are. The revision made the clause wish “the singer’s godless foes to die away”. The refuter found that the hymn never calls the others godless or foes: “godless” comes from the hatreds of 8.39.2, and the identification as enemies is Sāyaṇa’s own gloss on 8.39.1, which Wilson follows, so the revision put the commentator’s reading into the plain sense against rule 2. He offered a sentence that sets the refrain beside the hymn’s own “Away let the destroyers go” without naming them, and the second round took it (below).

Day 88, cleared. The critic found gharma, pratiprasthātṛ and adhvaryu standing unglossed for a reader at six in the morning. The revision shortened the context clause to “Midway through a hymn to Agni, the verse speaks in numbers”, glossed gharma in place as a pot of boiling milk (a note in the Wilson volume gives the gharma as the earthen vessel used to boil milk in the Pravargya), and dropped pratiprasthātṛ, calling the two by his own dual adhvaryū, glossed as priests. The refuter reopened the packet: seven ṛtvijaḥ milk the one gharma, two adhvaryus direct (prayojayataḥ) five others, and the five are the sacrificer, the brahman, the hotṛ, the āgnīdhra and the prastotṛ; he judged the appositive a fair gloss of the technical term and cleared it, with one nit in the evidence only: the note is in Cowell’s hand in volume 5, not Wilson’s. This revision shipped on day 88 at the first edit; the fourth round replaced it (below).

Day 96, refuted. The critic found “Closing a hymn to Soma” inexact, since 9.58 has four verses and the key is verse 3, and the image without its point. The revision opened “Near the close of a short hymn to Soma” and gave the point, but dropped “cites the Śāṭyāyanaka” and made the whole story the object of “takes”. The refuter found that the poison, the four verse hymn and the gain counted as taken, not accepted, all stand inside Sāyaṇa’s quotation of the Śāṭyāyanaka about Taranta and Purumīḷha, whom he sets beside Avatsāra as a parallel, so the revision gave a Brāhmaṇa narrative as his own reading. He offered a sentence that restores the citation and keeps the critic’s point, and the second round took it (below).

Traps. These belong in traps.md. From day 87: Sāyaṇa’s preface to a hymn often restates the Anukramaṇī, opening with atrānukramaṇikā; the seers and deities it lists are the index’s tradition, not his reading, so never credit him with them, and never move a detail from the preface onto a verse. From days 70 and 84: the context clause must come from Griffith’s text, not from Sāyaṇa’s gloss on another verse of the same hymn; his ritual frame (the gods summoned to the rite) and his identifications (the others of a refrain as enemies) slip into the plain sense easily, and a word from a neighbouring verse (the godless of 8.39.2) must not be fused onto a refrain that does not carry it. From day 84: many hymns carry a refrain wishing that enemies or rivals die; state it as the text does, since “away” for “die” is softening under rule 6. From days 70, 89 and 92: keep the shape of his gloss; a compound such as sarvadevātmaka means comprising, not the self of; where he gives alternatives with vā, carry both or say which; and where he adds a word (manasā eva śraddadhānaḥ) he keeps the verse’s word as well. From day 96: the voice rule asks the context clause to name the god, and under a printed key verse a clause that opens on “its” with no antecedent reads as the verse itself; and where Sāyaṇa quotes a Brāhmaṇa after tathā ca, the story is the Brāhmaṇa’s, so the sentence must say he cites it and never make it the object of his reading. From days 66 and 75: where Sāyaṇa’s gloss ends with a Nirukta reference (niru.), the reading is Yāska’s first; open Sarup at that section and name Yāska. From day 75: the packet’s Yāska section can miss a verse the Nirukta does treat (it reported nothing on 7.100.6, which Nirukta 5.8 quotes and glosses); search Sāyaṇa’s Sanskrit on the key verse for a Nirukta mark before trusting the packet’s silence, and read Sarup’s exegetical notes to learn which sentence is Yāska’s own and which an earlier authority’s he reports. From day 88: Wilson’s manuscript breaks off in 8.44, and from there the translation and notes of volume 5 are Cowell’s; an evidence gloss should name the hand the packet gives. From day 88 again: a gloss on a technical ritual term (gharma as a pot of boiling milk) taken from a translator’s note is the conduit’s, not Sāyaṇa’s; gloss the term from his own words elsewhere in the hymn or its viniyoga (here gharmayāga and abhiṣṭave gharmadohe), or give it in the as given in form with a hedge. From the second and third rounds on day 88: a revision that answers a critic’s narrow point (the place form) inherits every fault a refuter has already found in the entry; a reviser should read the earlier refutations of the same entry and mend them too, or the revision is refuted again and the older wording ships, as it did here twice before the fourth round mended both. And from day 84 in the fourth round: a context clause whose subject carries a restrictive relative clause ending in a verb (“that bids the destroyers go wishes”) reads as a garden path at six in the morning; set the clause off with commas or recast it. And from day 84 in the fifth round: a context clause that describes a hymn’s refrain or a neighbouring verse without placing the key verse fails rule 2 even when every word of it is true; say where the verse stands and what it turns from or to.
