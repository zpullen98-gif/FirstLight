## The Rig Veda, days 1 to 32 (veda-01)

Sixty four candidates were proposed, a primary and a standby for each of the thirty two days, in seven lanes: five of six days (L1 to L5, with L4 carrying days 19, 20 and 22 to 25), one of a single day (L6, day 32), and one sensitive lane of a single day (S1, day 21, the horse sacrifice). The proposals for days 16 to 18 and 28 to 31 came from second runs of lanes L3 and L5, the first runs having returned only part of their days. All thirty two primaries were judged at first judgement, each after the verifier ran verse.js on the key, reopened Sāyaṇa’s Sanskrit in the packet at the verse named, checked Wilson’s translation and note where the packet gave them, and confirmed the date of the pinned Wikisource revision. Twenty eight were VERIFIED and four CORRECTED (days 9, 10, 29 and 31). Each primary was then attacked by a refuter who reopened the packet source. One was refuted, day 5, for drift; it was judged again, CORRECTED, and lived. Five critics then read the batch in turn. The first raised nine problems and eight days were revised (2, 7, 8, 11, 12, 26, 29 and 30); the second raised five and fourteen days were revised (2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 17, 18, 19, 22 and 32); the third raised two and two days were revised (8 and 27); the fourth raised two and two days were revised (2 and 12); the fifth raised three and three days were revised (7, 8 and 21). Every revision was CORRECTED by its reviser and attacked by a fresh refuter who reopened the packet. Of twenty nine revisions, twenty eight were cleared and one was refuted, the second revision of day 2. Status gives thirty two living primaries, and all thirty two survive and are used, twenty of them in a revised form (days 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17, 18, 19, 21, 22, 26, 27, 29, 30 and 32). The thirty two standbys were never verified, because no primary fell.

This is the second edit, after the fifth critic’s revisions. The editor read everything again from disk, ran status.js afresh, and changed nothing in any entry: every entry in batch.json is copied exactly as status.js gives its survivor, and none held a straight quote to be made curly. Against the first edit, days 2, 7, 8, 12 and 21 differ: days 2 and 12 by the fourth round of revisions and days 7, 8 and 21 by the fifth, both below. An earlier run of this second edit had been written before the fifth round landed; this one replaces it, and against it only days 7, 8 and 21 differ.

The spread. Sāyaṇa is named on thirty one days, thirty of them alone. Yāska is named on two days: alone on day 2, on 1.22.17, where he gives two teachers’ views of the three steps; and on day 22 beside Sāyaṇa, where on 1.164.46 Sāyaṇa reads the One as the Sun, not other than Brahman and so the self of all, and Yāska, Nirukta 7.18, reads it as this very Agni, the great self; that difference is named, not resolved. No entry is hedged: every reading of Sāyaṇa is taken from his own Sanskrit in the Vedārthaprakāśa as the packet gives it from the pinned revisions of Sanskrit Wikisource, glossed by the proposer and checked by the verifier, with Wilson’s translation used only to confirm it; Yāska on days 2 and 22 is from Lakshman Sarup’s Nirukta (1920 to 1921), and on day 2 also from the Sanskrit of the Nirukta that Sāyaṇa quotes on the verse. The gate still warns that Sāyaṇa holds nearly every day of the set this batch joins (ninety two of ninety four at this edit). That follows from the roster, where Sāyaṇa alone covers every verse and Yāska treats only some. The fourth critic listed the key verses of this batch that the Nirukta treats (1.1.1, 1.22.17, 1.32.1, 1.41.9, 1.84.15, 1.115.1, 1.164.46, 1.170.1, 1.181.4, 1.185.1 and 2.38.4); on most of them Sāyaṇa quotes Yāska and agrees with him, so a second name would not be a genuine difference, as the refuter of day 10 found for 1.84.15 at Nirukta 4.25. Eight of the unverified standbys (days 1, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17 and 27) name Yāska, and anyone who wants more of him should start there.

The corrected entries at first judgement. Day 9, on 1.72.6: the reading (the thrice seven as twenty one sacrifices, seven pākayajñas, seven haviryajñas and seven somayajñas, laid in Agni alone because without him no sacrifice can be made) is Sāyaṇa’s word for word, and Wilson’s note agrees. Only the context clause changed: the proposal opened “Once the gods have found the hidden Agni”, but in the day’s text the gods do not find him (1.72.2) and it is the mortal band that finds Agni standing in the loftiest station (1.72.4); it was made to open “Once Agni is found in the loftiest station”, and the second round changed it again (below). Day 10, on 1.84.15: Sāyaṇa supplies the sun’s rays as the subject, glosses the name as their own light hidden at night, the verb as knew (ajānan), and the moon’s house as its orb, the rays reflected in the clear watery orb. The context clause was made to name the god, Indra, as the brief’s voice requires, and the reading was brought closer to his gloss; the second round took it closer again (below). Day 29, on 2.28.8: Griffith joins Sāyaṇa’s verses 1 and 2, so the app’s 2.28.8 is Sāyaṇa’s 2.28.9, and the source line says so (his verse 9) while the place stays the app’s verse, as the roster trap requires. The reading of the sins as debts, those made by forebears and by the speaker, holds; one phrase changed, “a debtor lying awake” to “a debtor awake before daylight”, because Sāyaṇa has him risen (utthāya) in the last part of the night (apararātreṣu), not lying in bed. Day 31, on 2.38.4: Sāyaṇa makes the weaver Night, like a woman weaving a garment, who wraps up again the light spread out (vitatam ālokam). “The light that the day spread out” became “the light that lay spread out”, since he names no agent of the spreading, and “at her loom” became “weaving cloth” (vastraṃ vayantī nārī).

The corrected entry after refutation. Day 5, on 1.41.9: the refuter found the evidence verbatim and the cowrie reading (kapardakān) Sāyaṇa’s own, Wilson’s note naming Sāyaṇa’s cowries and Yāska at Nirukta 3.16 having dice. The attack that landed was drift in the object of fear: the sentence made the shells dreaded by the other player, but Sāyaṇa makes the man holding them the one feared (dadato haste dhārayataḥ puruṣāt … bibhīyāt), the other player fearing whether he will win, and his comparison is that as that fear is, so evil speech should be feared. The second judgement upheld the refutation as a correction: the reading now says the shells’ holder is feared by the other player until they fall, as evil speech should be feared. The key, the ref, the source line and the evidence are unchanged.

The revisions after the first critic. Each reviser reopened the packet at the verse, confirmed the evidence verbatim, and ran verse.js and the entry check; each revision kept the key, the ref, the commentator and the source line, and each was attacked by a refuter who reopened the packet and found nothing to refute.

Day 2, on 1.22.17: the sentence spelled the god “Vishnu” twice under a key verse that prints Griffith’s “Visnu”, and day 20 writes “Visnu”. Both became “Visnu”. This form was later replaced (fourth round, below).

Day 7, on 1.52.9: “the Maruts as serving men in the form of breath” could be read as making the Maruts servants, where Sāyaṇa glosses nṛṣācaḥ as prāṇarūpeṇa nṝn sevamānāḥ, attending on men in the form of breath; it now says “attending on men”. The context clause was made to name the singers’ praise and the Maruts’ joy (Griffith’s “joyed”) and to open “Telling of Indra’s fight with Vrtra” to stay within the cap. The refuter noted that Sāyaṇa takes the Maruts’ joy causatively, as gladdening Indra, but the reading does not claim the joy, so there is no drift.

Day 8, on 1.62.3: the old order let “once Indra promised their milk to her young” attach to the Paṇis’ theft, where in Sāyaṇa’s tale the cows are carried off first (paṇibhir goṣv apahṛtāsu) and Saramā agrees to go only when Indra promises her young the milk of those cows. It now reads “sent after the cows the Paṇis stole, going only once Indra promised their milk to her young”.

Day 11, on 1.89.9: “the hymn turns to its length” let the length be the hymn’s. It now reads “the hymn names that life’s length”, tying it to the life appointed by the Gods in 1.89.8. The reading is unchanged.

Day 12, on 1.95.3: the reading gave the births as the fire beneath the ocean, the sun in heaven and the lightning in mid air, under a key verse that names mid air first and the waters last, so a reader could not square the two. The reading was made to name the lightning and to say that he takes “the waters” as a name for mid air; the clause on the sun marking the seasons was dropped to keep the cap, and the evidence was trimmed to the passage on the births. The fourth round finished this correction (below).

Day 26, on 2.1.13: the context clause doubled its word (“names Agni as each god in turn, this verse turns to the gods”). It now reads “the verse speaks of the gods”; the reading is unchanged.

Day 29, on 2.28.8 (his verse 9): in “the sins as debts, his forebears’ and his own” the nearest antecedent of “his” was Sāyaṇa. His gloss is the debts made by fathers and others (pitrādibhiḥ kṛtāni) and those made by the speaker (mayā niṣpāditāni); it now reads “the singer’s forebears’ and his own”. The correction at first judgement (awake before daylight) stands.

Day 30, on 2.30.1: “the time of their first creation being no time at all” reads in English as instant creation, the opposite of his point: he takes kim in dismissal (kiṃśabda ākṣepe), no measure of the time of their birth at all (na kim api), since the waters are eternal (nityā eva hi tāḥ). It now reads that he takes the question as a dismissal, since the waters are eternal and there was no first time to measure. Wilson’s note gives the same drift.

The first critic’s ninth problem, on the prose of the batch, was that fifteen context clauses used the formula “turns to”; it was not revised as such in the first round, and it came back from the second critic.

The revisions after the second critic. Day 2, on 1.22.17, REFUTED. The revision added Yāska beside Sāyaṇa in the two commentator form, with the three steps as earth, mid air and heaven, or sunrise, the meridian and sunset. The refuter found two faults. It drifted: Aurṇavābha, as Yāska gives him in the Sanskrit that Sāyaṇa quotes, names three places (samārohaṇe viṣṇupade gayaśirasi), which Sarup renders as the mountain of sunrise, the meridian and the mountain of sunset, and to turn the two mountains into times of day is Wilson’s own inference, not Yāska’s words. And it misstated the relation: rule 3 allows two commentators only where they genuinely differ, but Sāyaṇa on this verse says that it is explained so by Yāska (seyam ṛk yāskenaivaṃ vyākhyātā) and quotes the Nirukta passage as its explanation, so setting the two side by side presents part of Sāyaṇa’s own adopted gloss as another man’s alternative.

Day 4, on 1.32.1: “Vritra” became “Vrtra”, matching day 7 and the spelling at 1.33.13; hymn 1.32 in the day’s text prints Griffith’s “Vṛitra”, which the sentence had not matched either. Cleared.

Day 7, on 1.52.9: “the hymn turns to” became “the hymn recalls”, since the verse looks back, between the smiting of Vrtra (1.52.8) and the Dragon’s roar (1.52.10), on praise raised and the Maruts’ joy. Cleared.

Day 8, on 1.62.3: “the hymn turns to” became “the hymn tells of the search”; 1.62.2 has the Angirases, knowing the places, find the cattle. Cleared.

Day 9, on 1.72.6: the context clause now reads “With Agni found in the loftiest station, the hymn speaks of what lies hidden in him”. What is hidden here is the things within him, the verse’s own guhyāni (Griffith’s mystic things), so it is the verse’s sense and not Sāyaṇa’s reading restated. To stay at forty five words, “of cooked food, of oblation and of Soma” became “of cooked food, oblation and Soma”. Cleared.

Day 10, on 1.84.15: “the verse turns to the Moon” became “the verse follows the tale of Dadhyac’s bones and the horse’s head” (1.84.13 and 1.84.14), and the reading became the sun’s rays recognising in the Moon’s clear watery orb their own light, hidden by night, which keeps his verb (amanvata glossed ajānan) and his place, the house as the orb (gṛhe maṇḍale). The refuter confirmed that Sāyaṇa quotes Yāska for the rays and their concealed name, but the watery orb is his own addition, so naming Sāyaṇa is right. Cleared.

Day 12, on 1.95.3: “the verse turns to” became “the verse counts his births”, since the verse names three births and Sāyaṇa goes through them one by one (asyāgnes trīṇi janmāni saṃbhavanti). Cleared.

Day 13, on 1.105.16: “the singer turns to” became “the singer points to the Sun’s road”, since the verse opens asau yaḥ panthā, that yonder path. Cleared. The refuter noted a looseness that did not land: Sāyaṇa glosses the path as the Sun himself, while the sentence says “a road” after Griffith; the closing clause ties it to the Sun.

Day 14, on 1.113.11: “the singer turns to” became “the singer counts the generations who see her”, since the verse sets side by side those who saw earlier dawns, the living, and those who will see her. Cleared.

Day 17, on 1.124.2: the context clause now says the hymn weighs Dawn’s dealings with Gods and men, after the kindled fire and Savitar sending creatures to work (1.124.1). The refuter noted that Sāyaṇa offers a second reading of yugāni and that choosing his first is not drift. Cleared.

Day 18, on 1.131.3: the context clause now says the hymn tells of those who worship him, after Heaven and Earth are bowed to Indra (1.131.1). The reading and its limit, that a wife shares the rite with her husband and does not perform it alone, are unchanged; the refuter confirmed that the men only view is the objection he rejects, and that he cites Manu 5.155 for the limit. Day 19, on 1.140.12: the context clause now says the hymn closes on prayers for gifts, which holds for its last four verses (1.140.10 to 1.140.13). Cleared at forty five words, the cap.

Day 22, on 1.164.46: “the singer turns to” became “the singer runs through the names of the gods”, since the verse lists them. The two readings and their places are unchanged; the refuter confirmed that Sāyaṇa himself reports that others take the verse of Agni and quotes Nirukta 7.18, and that Sun against Agni is a genuine difference, which is why the two commentator form stands here though it fell on day 2. Day 32, on 3.2.9: “the hymn turns to” became “the hymn praises his threefold fire”; “Sāyaṇa, commentary on 3.2.9” became “Sāyaṇa, on 3.2.9”, as every other day has it; and the source line gained “on” before the verse. The reading, his three bodies, earthly, lightning and solar, or else the rite’s three fuel sticks (the two āghāra and one anūyāja), is unchanged. Cleared.

Day 2, on 1.22.17, now Yāska alone. The critic raised the spread: Sāyaṇa alone on thirty one days, while on this verse the Nirukta has a comment with its own content, two teachers’ views of where the three steps are planted, distinct from Sāyaṇa’s own gloss of Visnu in his descent as Trivikrama; the refuted second revision had failed by setting the two men side by side, which naming Yāska alone avoids. The entry now names Yāska, Nirukta 12.19, who explains the three steps by two teachers’ views: on earth, mid air and heaven (Śākapūṇi), or on the mountain of sunrise, the meridian and the mountain of sunset (Aurṇavābha). The mountains stay places, in Sarup’s order, as the refutation of the second round required; the critic’s suggested wording had reordered them and added “between”, and the reviser kept Sarup’s order instead. The evidence is Sarup’s English at 12.19 as the packet gives it (OCR as printed) and the Sanskrit that Sāyaṇa quotes as Yāska’s (pṛthivyām antarikṣe divīti śākapūṇiḥ samārohaṇe viṣṇupade gayaśirasīty aurṇavābhaḥ), which confirms the attribution. The sensitive day. Day 21, on 1.162.21, a passage on killing in the hymn of the horse sacrifice: the plain sense states in one plain clause that the horse, now killed, is addressed, true to the hatchet and the dissection of 1.162.18 to 20. The reading gives Sāyaṇa’s own answer to the objection that death and the loss of limbs are seen: the horse does not die like other horses (itarāśvavat), is not harmed to no purpose (vyarthahiṃsāyāḥ abhāvāt), and reaches the gods by the paths of the gods. The key stops before the second half of the verse, on the yoked steeds. It was VERIFIED at first judgement and not refuted; its sentence was raised by none of the five critics, and the fifth changed only its source line, into the batch’s form (above).

Traps. These observations may belong in traps.md. From day 5: when Sāyaṇa glosses a verb of fearing (bibhīyāt with the ablative), the ablative names what is feared; in a simile check who fears whom before the English says it. From day 9: the context clause must not bring in a myth the day’s text does not tell (the gods finding a hidden Agni), even when the myth is well known from other hymns. From day 29: Sāyaṇa’s circumstantial glosses (risen, in the last part of the night) are part of his reading, and a natural English image (lying awake) can misstate them; and the roster trap on the hymns where Sāyaṇa counts a verse more than Griffith worked as written, the verifier confirming the match on the Sanskrit words themselves and not on the verse number. From day 12: where Sāyaṇa’s gloss assigns Griffith’s words to different places (Griffith’s mid air his ocean, Griffith’s waters his mid air), the reading must name the whole mapping, every place that moves, or it will seem to contradict the key printed above it; naming only one of the two moves took three rounds to find. From day 29 again: after the semicolon a bare “his” attaches to the commentator; name the singer or the speaker. From day 30: a Sanskrit negation of measure (na kim api) turned into an English idiom (no time at all) can say the opposite of what he means; keep to his logic in plain words. From day 2: where Sāyaṇa quotes Yāska as the explanation of the verse (yāskenaivaṃ vyākhyātā), the two are not two readings, and the two commentator form misstates them; Yāska may stand beside Sāyaṇa only where Sāyaṇa reports him as another view, as on day 22, but he may stand alone where his passage has content of its own, and Sāyaṇa’s quotation of it then confirms the attribution. From day 2 again: Wilson’s notes on Yāska carry Wilson’s own inferences (the three steps as sunrise, meridian and sunset, and so Visnu as the Sun); read Yāska in his Sanskrit or in Sarup before saying what he says, and keep Sarup’s places as places. From day 4: where the day’s text prints a name in two spellings (Griffith’s Vṛitra in 1.32, Vrtra at 1.33.13), the batch should keep one spelling across all its days. From days 8 and 27: a word used in the context clause and used again after the semicolon (through and through, people and people) can blur the reading or make it seem to speak of the same persons; read the two halves together for a doubled word before the entry goes up. From days 7 and 8: a Sanskrit term or name carried over from Sāyaṇa’s gloss keeps its diacritics by habit (Bṛhat Sāman, Paṇis), while the batch writes Griffith’s plain forms (Vrtra, Visnu, Sarama, Panis); where the app’s Griffith prints the name, write it as he does, and write any other term plainly. From day 7 again: a context clause that names the two things the key verse does, in its order, is a line by line paraphrase however it is worded; place the verse by what stands before and after it instead.
