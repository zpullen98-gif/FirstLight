## The Tanakh, days 31 to 60 (tanakh-02)

Sixty candidates were proposed, a primary and a standby for each of the thirty days (Exodus 40 to Deuteronomy 13), in six lanes (days 31 to 35; 36 to 40; 41 to 45; 46 to 50; 51 to 53, 55 and 56; and 57, 59 and 60) and one lane for the sensitive days (54 and 58). All thirty primaries were judged at first judgement: twenty seven VERIFIED and three CORRECTED, days 34, 44 and 56. Each was then attacked by a refuter who reopened the commentator’s Hebrew through packet.js on the key verse, found the evidence words verbatim, checked the context clause against day.js and ran verse.js and the entry check again. Two primaries were refuted, days 43 and 45, each on its reading (drift and attribution in the second commentator’s half, Ramban on both days); each was judged again with the refutation in hand, CORRECTED, and lived. A critic then read the entries as a reader would, four times in all. The first run held five: days 34, 41, 44 and 53 on their wording, and day 42 on its key verse. The second held day 42 again, because the app’s text had not yet been repaired, and day 46 on its syntax. After the first edit the third run held three: day 42, then a hole, day 44 as not the best line of its day, and day 31 on the syntax of its Ibn Ezra half. After the second round of revisions the fourth run held day 44 once more, because the move to Numbers 6:26 had been refuted on form alone. Ten revisions were made in three rounds, and every revision that changed an entry was attacked a second time by a refuter; one of them, the second revision of day 44, was refuted, and the third revision of day 44 repaired it and was not. Status gives thirty living primaries, and all thirty are used; there is no hole. The thirty standbys were never verified, because no primary died. The editor changed nothing in any entry: every entry is copied exactly as status.js gives its survivor, the revised wording on days 31, 34, 41, 42, 44, 46 and 53 and the corrected wording on days 43, 45 and 56 included, and no key, sentence or source line held a straight quote to be made curly. Against the first edit, the only entry that changed in this second edit is day 44. The candidate gate passes with no error.

The spread. Rashi is named on twenty four of the thirty days, thirteen of them alone (35, 36, 40, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50, 52, 54, 57, 58 and 60) and eleven beside a second commentator (31, 32, 34, 38, 42, 43, 44, 45, 51, 55 and 56). Ramban is named on five days, alone on two (41 and 59) and as the second voice beside Rashi on three (38, 43 and 45). Sforno is named on four days, alone on day 37 and beside Rashi on 32, 51 and 55. Ibn Ezra is named on three days, each beside Rashi (31, 42 and 56). The Midrash is named on three days, by collection and section: Leviticus Rabbah 9:7 on day 33, Leviticus Rabbah 30:12 on day 39 and Numbers Rabbah 21:16 on day 53. Rashbam is named on two days, beside Rashi on days 34 and 44. Radak and Metzudat David are not named; neither has coverage in Leviticus, Numbers or Deuteronomy. Eleven days name two commentators, and on each the difference is named and not resolved. No entry is hedged: every reading is taken from the commentator’s own Hebrew in Sefaria’s export at commit 3f10136, reached through packet.js, and the plan has no conduits. The third revision of day 44 changed its commentators, from Ibn Ezra alone to Rashi and Rashbam, which cost Ibn Ezra his only day alone. The candidate gate warns that Rashi holds 43 of the 60 days of the plan so far (nineteen in the first batch and twenty four here); the warning is the brief’s default order at work and not an error, though the next batch might look harder for Ramban, Sforno and Ibn Ezra where their comment on the chosen verse is fuller.

The corrected entries at first judgement. Day 34, on Leviticus 10:3, a sensitive matter (the death of Nadab and Abihu), Rashi and Rashbam: the proposal had called Rashi’s reading a consolation, but that word is not in Rashi’s comment; it is Rashbam’s own description of the aggadah he sets aside. The Rashi clause was made to give what his words carry, from the Sifra: Moses telling Aaron he had expected the sanctuary to be hallowed through himself or Aaron and now sees the sons as greater; Rashbam’s charge not to mourn or leave the service is his own, and the two are named without resolving. Day 44, on Numbers 6:7, Ibn Ezra: the proposal made the derivation of nazir from the crown his own, but he reports it from others (ויש אומרים) and judges it not far fetched (ואיננו רחוק), while on 6:2 he himself derives the word from separation; the sentence was made to say he takes the reported derivation as not far fetched, and to keep the remark on the true king, which is his own (ודע). That entry no longer lands: the third revision moved day 44 to 6:26. Day 56, on Deuteronomy 1:1, Rashi and Ibn Ezra: the context clause called the book Moses’ last words, which the day’s text does not say, so it now reads Moses’ address to the people in the fortieth year (1:3); and Ibn Ezra’s places lost the word real, which was the proposer’s emphasis, since he says only מקומות.

The corrected entries after refutation. Day 43, on Numbers 3:1, Rashi and Ramban: Rashi’s half stood (whoever teaches another’s son Torah is as if he begot him). The sentence had Ramban reading the named sons as Aaron’s alone, and alone is not in him: by the plain sense he takes the generations of Moses as the Amramite family named below (3:27), and then closes the same comment by keeping the midrash as the verse’s hint (והדרש סמכוהו ... לרמוז), so the sentence staged a sharper opposition than he draws. The second judgement removed alone, gives Ramban by the plain sense as Aaron’s priests, Moses’ line being the Amramites named below, keeping that teaching as a hint, trimmed the context clause to hold fifty nine words, and extended the evidence to Ramban’s closing words. Day 45, on Numbers 8:2, Rashi and Ramban: Rashi’s half stood (God consoling Aaron, grieved at having no part in the princes’ dedication, with the task of the lamps). The sentence had Ramban reading the charge itself as alluding to the Hanukkah lights; but that allusion is Ramban explaining the aggadah Rashi quotes (אבל ענין ההגדה הזו, לדרוש רמז מן הפרשה), and his own plain reading of the passage is a different one, that it completes the laws of the lamps. The second judgement took the refuter’s wording, so that Ramban takes that midrash as alluding to the Hanukkah lights of Aaron’s Hasmonean descendants, kept even after the Temple’s destruction, and dropped greater to hold fifty nine words.

The first round of revisions. Day 34, on Leviticus 10:3: the critic found that the two commentator form had broken, since Rashi heard Moses telling Aaron with no as, so that the elided Rashbam clause, as a charge, had nothing to attach to and could be read as though Moses were the charge; and greater had lost what the sons are greater than (Rashi’s ממני וממך, than me and than you). The reviser reopened both comments and restored the form of rule 1: Rashi hears it as Moses saying he had expected the sanctuary to be hallowed through himself or Aaron and now finds the sons greater than both, and Rashbam as a charge not to mourn or leave the service. It holds sixty words; commentators, places and evidence are unchanged, and the second refuter found both halves verbatim and the difference genuine, since Rashbam rejects that aggadah as not the plain sense. Day 41, on Leviticus 26:6, Ramban: the reading stranded the land between commas, so a reader could not tell that the land, while the commandments are kept, being as the world before Adam’s sin is Ramban’s reason for the beasts ceasing to harm. The revision joins it with since and drops was to hold forty five words; the second refuter confirmed that Ramban sets Rabbi Yehudah against Rabbi Shimon, calls the second הנכון, and grounds it in the world before the first man’s sin, when no beast killed a man. Day 44, on Numbers 6:7, Ibn Ezra: the true king clause hung as an absolute after the reported derivation, so it could be read as part of the view he reports rather than his own remark. The revision gave it to him with his own verb, calls, kept the crown (his נזר ועטרת מלכות בראשו), and said the world’s desires for his תאות העולם; it held forty five words, and the second refuter judged calls a report of his own words and not a forbidden verb. This first revision is superseded by the third. Day 46, on Numbers 9:7, Rashi: the second critic run found that the since clause hung on fit to come through Moses, so the sentence read as if Moses’ merit were the reason; in Rashi the section was fit to be said through Moses like all the Torah (כשאר כל התורה), but (אלא) these men merited that it be said through them, because merit is brought about through the worthy (שמגלגלין זכות ע״י זכאי). The reviser took the critic’s fix with one change toward the Hebrew, said through them in place of given through them, so the since clause now attaches to the men; it holds forty five words, and the second refuter found the evidence closing the comment verbatim. Day 53, on Numbers 28:2, the Midrash: the context clause had Moses asking for a shepherd, which is Numbers 27:15 to 17 and belongs to day 52, and the sequence it implied was the link Rashi and Numbers Rabbah 21:2 draw, not the text’s own. The new clause is taken from the day itself: the LORD charging Moses at the opening of a calendar of offerings for day, sabbath, new moon and feast (chapters 28 and 29). The reading, Numbers Rabbah 21:16, is unchanged; the second refuter noted that feast is loose beside the fast of 29:7 but not wrong enough to refute. Day 42, on Numbers 2:2, Rashi and Ibn Ezra: the critic held the key verse because the app’s text printed fathers’houses with no space (a fault of transcription in Sefaria’s JPS text; the 1917 translation prints the space) and because it opens a quotation mark that closes only at 2:31. The reviser kept the entry unchanged, since rule 8 forbids mending the key and the repair belonged to the app’s text; the first edit then left day 42 as a hole, because the operator had meanwhile repaired the text (FirstLight commit 1fbdbd1, firstlight v90, 252 sites across the Tanakh) and the unchanged key no longer matched the day, which the candidate gate reported under rule 8.

The second round of revisions, after the first edit. Day 42: the reviser recopied the key from day.js as the repaired text now prints it, with fathers’ houses and the space, and changed nothing else; verse.js gives it ok at Numbers 2:2 (at 118, 2), the unclosed opening quotation mark stays as the translation’s own, and the sentence, commentators, source line and evidence are unchanged. The second refuter reopened both comments on באתת and found them verbatim: Rashi’s dyed cloth coloured like each tribe’s breastplate stone, and Ibn Ezra’s figures of man, lion, ox and eagle like the cherubim Ezekiel saw, which he gives as the ancients’ word (וקדמונינו אמרו), a form that adopts and does not distance, and the sentence says by the ancients’ account. It was not refuted, and day 42, a hole in the first edit, lands. Day 31, on Leviticus 2:1, Rashi and Ibn Ezra: the critic found the Ibn Ezra half hard to parse (the soul named read as a noun with a participle, and this had no clear antecedent). The revision reads as naming the soul because the meal offering is a freewill gift and the soul too is called willing, which is his own wording (והזכיר הנפש שהמנחה נדבה גם הנפש תקרא נדיבה); Rashi’s half, key and source line are unchanged, and the second refuter found both halves verbatim and did not refute it. Day 44: the critic held that the entry was not the best line of the day, since Numbers 6:26, the close of the Priestly Blessing, is a whole citable sentence, and the reading on 6:7 turns on nezer, crown, which the English consecration never shows, with half of it a derivation Ibn Ezra only reports. The reviser moved the entry to 6:26, Rashi (יכבש כעסו, He suppresses His anger) and Rashbam (He does not hide His face but turns to His beloved to be gracious). The second refuter found the reading sound and both comments verbatim, but refuted it on form: the half before the semicolon, Closing the blessing Aaron and his sons are to speak over Israel, had no main clause, so the one sentence was a fragment, and it did not say who gives the blessing or that the verse is its last line.

The third round of revisions, after the fourth critic run. Day 44: the critic held the day again, since the 6:7 entry that the first edit had landed still carried the objection, and the 6:26 move had failed on form alone. The reviser landed the 6:26 entry with the refuter’s repaired context clause, a full main clause checked against day.js (6:22 the LORD speaks to Moses, 6:23 Aaron and his sons are to bless Israel, 6:24 to 26 the blessing, 6:26 its last line): The LORD gives Moses the blessing Aaron and his sons are to speak over Israel, and the verse is its closing line. The reading is unchanged from the second revision: Rashi, on 6:26, reads the lifted countenance as God suppressing His anger (יִכְבֹּשׁ כַּעֲסוֹ, Rosenbaum and Silbermann Hebrew), and Rashbam, on 6:26, as God not hiding His face but turning to His beloved to be gracious (שלא יסתיר פניו מכם ... אבל הוא ישא פנים שלו את אהובו שיפנה אליו לחננו, Daat Hebrew). It holds fifty two words, under the cap of sixty for two commentators; verse.js gives it ok at Numbers 6:26 (at 122, 26), and the entry check passes. The second refuter reopened both comments, found them verbatim and the difference genuine (anger restrained against a face turned in favour to the beloved), found the context clause now a main clause that repeats no four word run of the key and names no commentator, and did not refute it. Day 44 lands on Numbers 6:26 with Rashi and Rashbam, and the Ibn Ezra entry on 6:7 is retired. The standby for day 44 is on 6:27 and was never verified.

The holes. None. Every day has a living primary, and every entry passes the candidate gate.

The sensitive days. Day 54, on Numbers 31:2: the context clause states in one plain clause that Israel kills Midian’s men and that Moses orders every captive killed but the virgin girls (31:7 and 31:15 to 18), and Rashi’s reading carries the limit he sets on the command, Midian and not Moab, which joined from fear while Midian meddled in another’s quarrel; his second explanation (Ruth and Naamah) is not claimed. Day 58, on Deuteronomy 6:5: the context clause names the order in 7:1 to 2 that the seven nations be destroyed, in one plain clause, and Rashi’s three glosses (both inclinations, even at the cost of life, all one’s wealth) carry the reading word for word; his second explanations are left aside and not denied. Day 35, on Leviticus 13:46, states the exclusion of the one declared unclean without softening it, and gives Rashi’s reading with the rabbis (Arakhin 16b), as his own comment frames it. Day 34 remains a sensitive matter after its revision, and its context clause states the deaths of Nadab and Abihu in one plain clause.

Traps that belong in traps.md. First, a word that labels a reading may come from another commentator’s summary of it: consolation on Leviticus 10:3 is Rashbam’s name for the aggadah he rejects, not Rashi’s word (day 34). Second, Ibn Ezra’s ויש אומרים introduces a view he reports and weighs (here ואיננו רחוק), not his own; where he gives his own view at another verse, the sentence must not make the reported one his, and a remark of his own that follows (ודע) needs his own verb, or it reads as part of the reported view (day 44, the retired 6:7 entry). By contrast his וקדמונינו אמרו adopts the ancients’ word, and the sentence may give it as his reading by their account (day 42). Third, when Ramban writes ענין ההגדה הזו he is explaining the midrash Rashi quotes, and that is his reading of the midrash, not his reading of the verse; say takes that midrash as (day 45). Fourth, Ramban often closes a plain sense comment by keeping the midrash as a hint (והדרש סמכוהו); a sentence that sets him against Rashi must not drop that, or it stages an opposition he declines (day 43). Fifth, the English beside Sforno (Munk) and Ramban (Chavel) adds to the Hebrew; paraphrase the Hebrew. Sixth, in the two commentator form the first commentator’s verb must carry its as, so that the second, elided clause has something to attach to (day 34). Seventh, the context clause must stay inside the day’s own passage: a link a commentator draws to the preceding chapter, when that chapter is the previous day’s reading, is his and not the text’s (day 53). Eighth, a since clause attaches to the nearest clause before it: where Rashi turns on אלא (fit for Moses, but through them), the sentence must carry the but, or his reason lands on the wrong party (day 46). Ninth, Sefaria’s JPS text dropped the space after a plural possessive (fathers’houses, goats’hair, Moses’face); the app’s text is now repaired (commit 1fbdbd1), and any key proposed, verified or kept unchanged against the old text is stale and must be recopied from day.js through a revision before it lands, as day 42 was. Tenth, the context clause must be a clause: a participial opening (Closing the blessing ...) needs a subject and a main verb after it before the semicolon, or the sentence is a fragment; the gate does not test syntax, and the refuter must test it (day 44, second revision). Eleventh, a reading whose point turns on a Hebrew word the English does not show (nezer, crown, under consecration) leaves the reader without its point; prefer a verse whose English carries what the commentator reads (day 44). Twelfth, a revision refuted on form alone, with its reading and evidence found sound, can come back through a further revision carrying the refuter’s repaired clause, and should, where the critic judges it the better line; the editor may not make that repair himself (day 44, third revision).
