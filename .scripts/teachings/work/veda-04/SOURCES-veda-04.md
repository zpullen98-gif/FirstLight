## The Rig Veda, days 97 to 126 (veda-04)

Sixty candidates were proposed, a primary and a standby for each of the thirty days, in seven lanes, and the work is read in Ralph Griffith’s translation. The first run of lane L1 proposed only days 97 and 98, so days 99 to 102 were proposed in a second run (L1r); days 116 and 117 (10.80 to 10.89, taking in the marriage hymn and the Vṛṣākapi hymn) went to the sensitive lane S1. Fifty two of the candidates named Sāyaṇa and eight named Yāska, all eight of them standbys (days 104, 105, 106, 119, 120, 121, 124 and 125). All thirty primaries were verified: twenty four as VERIFIED at first judgement and six (days 97, 109, 119, 121, 122 and 125) as CORRECTED. Each primary was then attacked by a refuter who reopened the packet source. Three were refuted (days 115, 123 and 126); each was judged a second time, CORRECTED, and lived. A critic then read the whole batch in four runs. The first raised five entries (days 97, 99, 115, 118 and 126) and one point on the batch as a whole; the second raised day 110 and the same batch point again; the third raised day 110 once more; the fourth raised the source line of day 110 and the wording of day 122. Nine revisions were written in all: one for each of the five entries of the first run, three for day 110 and one for day 122. Each was judged CORRECTED and then attacked again. Eight were not refuted; the first revision of day 110 was refuted, its second revision was not and landed, and its third revision, which changed only the source line of the second, was not refuted and is the one that stands. Seven days therefore land in revised form: 97, 99, 110, 115, 118, 122 and 126. Status gives thirty living primaries, and all thirty are used. The thirty standbys were never verified, because no primary fell. The editor changed nothing in any entry: every entry is copied exactly as status.js gives its survivor (for the seven revised days, the revised entry), and none held a straight quote to be made curly. The candidate gate passes with no error and one warning: counted with the thirty two veda days already landed, Sāyaṇa holds sixty one of the sixty two days. In this batch he is the first commentator on twenty nine days, Yāska stands beside him on day 110 and alone on day 115, and no other commentator survives unused, so the warning asks for nothing the editor may do.

The spread. Sāyaṇa is named on twenty nine days (every day but 115) and Yāska on two (day 110, Nirukta 5.25, beside Sāyaṇa, and day 115, Nirukta 1.20, alone); one day, 110, names two commentators. No entry is hedged. Every Sāyaṇa reading is taken from his own Sanskrit on Sanskrit Wikisource, in the pinned revision the packet names for each hymn, with Wilson’s translation (volumes 5 and 6 on archive.org) used as a check that the gloss is his; both Yāska readings are taken from Lakshman Sarup’s edition and translation of the Nirukta (archive.org nighantuniruktao00yask, the full text matching the pinned copy by checksum), and on day 110 Sāyaṇa himself quotes Yāska at the place cited. Where Webster’s bracketed notes in Wilson were consulted (days 105 and 113), they served only as a report of Sāyaṇa beside his own words, never in place of them. Several verifiers and refuters confirmed the year of each Wikisource revision from the Wikisource API, since the page itself does not print it.

The CORRECTED entries at first judgement. Day 97: the proposal had Sāyaṇa read the Two as the “bright and dark fortnights” and the gods drink Soma’s “nectar”; his words are pūrvāparapakṣau, the former and latter fortnights, and kalāḥ pibanti, one by one, so the sentence was made to read “the fortnights” and “the gods drink him digit by digit”, and the source line names the page and the year (a revision below changed the reading again). He also offers a second reading (the two names aṃśu and Soma); the sentence gives his first. Day 109: the proposal said the counsel was declared by Savitṛ “as maker of” revealed and remembered teaching, which turns an epithet into a reason; Sāyaṇa gives śrutismṛtikartā only in apposition, so it now reads “Savitṛ, maker of revealed and remembered teaching”. Day 119: the context clause paraphrased the key verse’s own first line, against rule 2; it now gives context only, a healer calling on the Plants to free his patient before the Plants themselves speak. Day 121: the context clause said the Guerdon was given to priests, which comes from Sāyaṇa’s introduction to the hymn (ṛtvigbhyo dīyamānā dakṣiṇā), not from Griffith’s text; it now speaks of the sacrificial Guerdon and those who give it. Day 122: the proposal made all of Sāyaṇa’s accounts of kasmai explain Ka as a name, but in the first he keeps the interrogative itself, pointing to Prajāpati because his nature is not known; the sentence now separates the pronoun from the name (a revision below changed its context clause). Day 125: the context clause was a participial fragment with no main verb; it now says the verse honours Faith in the rite, and the reading lost two words to stay within forty five. On day 125 Sāyaṇa also gives an alternative (the fire kindled by the seer Śraddhā), which the sentence does not deny.

The refutations and the second judgements. Day 115 was refuted on drift and attribution: the context clause named the man called dull in friendship from the first half of 10.71.5 and the reading then said Sāyaṇa takes “this man” as a mere reciter; but in Sāyaṇa the first half’s man (sthirapīta) is the knower of meaning and is praised (arthajñaḥ praśastaḥ), and only the second half blames the reciter (uttarārdhena kevalapāṭhako nindyate), so the reader was told the opposite of his reading of that man. The second judgement changed the context clause to “one who hears in vain” and the reading to “the man who wanders”, which is the figure the key verse holds. Day 123 was refuted on form and on the plain sense: the reading quoted six words of the key verse in two quotations, over rule 3’s three (the entry check tests each quotation alone, not the total), and the context clause said the hymn asks what was before existent and non existent, when 10.129.1 says that neither then was and 10.129.6 asks whence creation came. The second judgement kept only “its own nature” in quotation, wrote “the One” for the other, and made the clause say what the hymn says. The reading itself, tat as the reality of Brahman known throughout the Vedānta and svadhā as māyā, undivided from it, held. Day 126 was refuted on drift and attribution: the reading named the agent of the creative fervour as Brahmā, but brahmaṇā is the instrumental of both the masculine Brahmā and the neuter brahman, Sāyaṇa does not choose, and his own proof texts (Taittirīya Āraṇyaka 8.6 and Muṇḍaka 1.1.9) point to the Self or Brahman; the god Brahmā and “before creation” come only from Wilson’s note, and the entry carried no hedge. The second judgement dropped the named agent and “before”. In all three the key, the reference and the evidence words were unchanged at that stage.

The revisions of the first critic run. Five, each reopening the packet (or, on day 115, the Nirukta) before changing a word, and each attacked again afterwards and not refuted. Day 97: the reading ended on “the gods drink him digit by digit”, and digit is the lunar term for a kalā in a sentence that never names the moon. The revision reads the Two as “the waxing and waning fortnights” (his vṛddhihrāsa of the former and latter pakṣa) and has the gods “drink his portions” (kalāḥ pibanti); it declined the critic’s “a leaf a day”, because Sāyaṇa says ekaika, one at a time, not one a day, so “leaf by leaf” stays. Day 99: the reading gave “the dressed” the gloss Sāyaṇa gives to “which bear”; he glosses śṛtāsaḥ as paripakvāḥ, ripened, and vahantaḥ separately as yāgaṃ nirvahantaḥ, carrying the sacrifice through. The revision gives each gloss its own word: “the dressed who bear as the ripened, carrying the sacrifice through”, at forty five words. Day 115: the critic found that Sāyaṇa’s own commentary on 10.71.5 closes by sending the reader to Nirukta 1.20, and that his glosses there (the meaning of speech as its flower and fruit, the illusion as a mere likeness of speech) are Yāska’s; the packet’s list of Nirukta sections had missed the passage because its OCR loses entries. The reviser opened Sarup’s full text and found Yāska quoting the stanza and explaining the barren illusion as a symbol of speech that grants no desires, and the meaning of speech as its fruit and flower. Since Sāyaṇa follows Yāska and only narrows the man to a mere reciter, one commentator is named, Yāska, the source of the gloss; the reading now says he reads fruit and flower as the meaning of speech and the illusion as a semblance of speech, “mere reciter” is dropped as Sāyaṇa’s and not Yāska’s, and the source line names Nirukta 1.20 in Sarup’s edition (1921). The key and the context clause are unchanged. Day 118: “the world of all three times” rendered kālatraya too literally; Sāyaṇa’s own gloss is atītānāgatavartamānarūpaṃ jagat, so the reading now says “the world, past, present and to come”, and the second “the world” became “it” to hold forty five words. Day 126: the context clause was a participial phrase with no subject, so it read as if Sāyaṇa opened the hymn, and the closing participle had no subject either. The revision gives the clause its own subject (“The verse opens a hymn of origins ending with the Creator forming sun, moon, heaven and earth”) and ties the closing gloss to the fervour with “which he explains as contemplating what would be made” (tapaś cātra sraṣṭavyaparyālocanalakṣaṇam). It kept the refuter’s correction against the critic’s suggested wording: “for creation” (sṛṣṭyarthaṃ), not “before creation”, and no named agent; “three verse” and “of old” were cut to hold forty five words. The refuter of the revision noted a slight risk that the relative clause could be read as attaching to creation rather than to the fervour, not enough to refute.

The three revisions of day 110. The second critic found that Yāska treats the key verse of day 110 (10.44.6) at Nirukta 5.25, in the day’s own packet, that Sāyaṇa quotes him there, and that the two differ: Sāyaṇa commits to debt (ṛṇenaiva) and adds evildoing and the lack of Indra’s grace, while Yāska, in Sarup’s translation, leaves those who could not board the ship of sacrifice remaining in this very place, or in debt, or in this world. It also found the plain sense (“the singer turns to the fate of others”) too vague for an exclusion verse under rule 6. The first reviser reopened the packet, confirmed both readings in their own words, and wrote the two commentator form at fifty seven words, with a new context clause setting the first callers of the gods against those who failed to board and sank. Its refuter found the reading half sound and refuted it on the plain sense: that clause is a line by line paraphrase of the key verse printed just above it, against rule 2, and “callers” is the commentators’ agentive gloss (Sāyaṇa’s devānām āhvātāraḥ, Yāska’s “who invoked the gods”) where Griffith’s text says “invocation”, so the plain sense restated the commentators instead of the day’s text. That revision did not land, and the first edit carried day 110 as first verified, naming Sāyaṇa alone. The third critic run raised it again on both counts. The second reviser reopened the packet, kept the reading half (Sāyaṇa reads those left behind as evildoers without Indra’s grace, brought low by debt, from tvatprasādarahitā janāḥ, pāpakarmāṇo janāḥ, ṛṇenaiva and adhogatiṃ prāptāḥ; Yāska, Nirukta 5.25, as those who stayed in this very world, or in debt), and replaced the context clause with one taken from the hymn around the verse, as the refuter had asked: between the bidding of Indra to sit on the holy grass (10.44.5) and the wish that the evil hearted be far away (10.44.7), the singer sets two fates at the sacrifice side by side. It stands at fifty six words and adds Yāska as the second commentator. Its refuter reopened the packet and the Wikisource API, found every element in the two commentators’ own words, the difference named and not resolved, and the context clause context rather than paraphrase, with no four word run from the key; it was not refuted. The fourth critic run then found that its source line gave Sāyaṇa’s half without his work, against rule 9, which asks for the name, then the work and the place, then the edition and year. The third reviser changed the source line alone, to name the Vedārthaprakāśa (158 characters, within the 160 allowed), with the key, the sentence and the evidence unchanged and the evidence words found again in the packet; its refuter reopened the pinned revision and the wiki API (revision 328215, 2021) and did not refute it. Day 110 lands in that form: Sāyaṇa, Vedārthaprakāśa on Rig Veda 10.44.6, sa.wikisource revision 328215 (2021), and Yāska, Nirukta 5.25, in Sarup’s edition (1921). The difference between the two readings is slight but real, and the sentence does not rank them.

The revision of day 122. The fourth critic run found that the context clause, “a hymn whose verses, but the last, end on one question”, used “but” for “except” between commas, which a reader at six in the morning is likely to take as a contrast. The reviser changed it to “all but the last”, which says plainly that verses 1 to 9 close on the refrain and verse 10 does not; the reading, key, source line and evidence are unchanged, and the evidence was found again verbatim in the packet (Sāyaṇa on kasmai: kiṃśabdo ’nirjñātasvarūpatvāt prajāpatau vartate; kāmayata iti kaḥ; kaṃ sukham). Its refuter reopened the pinned revision (400427, 2024) and day.js, confirmed that verse 10 names Prajapati instead of asking, and noted that leaving out Sāyaṇa’s fourth account (the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa story of Indra and Prajāpati) is an omission, not a claim beyond his words; it was not refuted, and day 122 lands at forty four words.

The holes. There are none: all thirty days are used.

Two things for the operator. The first two critic runs asked for one form of source line across the batch, and no revision could give it, since a revision changes one entry and the editor may change none. The operator has ruled on it (rulings.json): src is never shown to the reader, since the shipped file holds key, ref, at, s and by, and each line names its commentator, the place and the edition read correctly, so the forms differ only in wording and nothing is revised. The fourth critic run’s point on day 110 was different in kind, since that line lacked the work altogether, and it went through a revision. For the record, days 97, 99 to 102 and 109 and 111 to 114 give the sūkta page title after sa.wikisource; days 98, 103 to 108 and 116 to 122 give “(the printed edition is not named)”; days 123 to 126 add a comma after Vedārthaprakāśa and give “(printed edition not named)”; day 115 cites the Nirukta in Sarup’s edition; and day 110 names Sāyaṇa with the Vedārthaprakāśa and Yāska with the Nirukta in one line. And the eight Yāska standbys were never verified; if a later pass wants Yāska named wherever the Nirukta treats a key verse, those standbys and Sāyaṇa’s own references to the Nirukta are the place to begin.

Traps. Seven belong in traps.md. From day 115: Sāyaṇa sometimes divides a verse between two figures, praising the one in the first half and blaming the one in the second; the context clause must not name the figure of the other half and then let the reading attach to him. Also from day 115: when Sāyaṇa closes a verse with a reference to the Nirukta (niru. with a section number, and anusaṃdheyam), his gloss is usually Yāska’s, and the packet’s list of Nirukta sections can miss the passage because its OCR loses entries; open Sarup at the section he names before crediting the gloss to Sāyaṇa alone. From day 126: brahmaṇā is ambiguous between Brahmā and brahman, and Sāyaṇa often leaves it so; do not name the god unless his words do, and never take Wilson’s unaccented “Brahma” or his “before creation” as Sāyaṇa’s. From day 121, as on day 70 in the batch before this one: Sāyaṇa’s introduction to a hymn is his frame, not Griffith’s text, and it must not enter the plain sense. From day 123: rule 3 caps quotation from the key verse at three words in all, across every quotation in the sentence, and the entry check counts each quotation alone, so the total must be counted by hand. From day 110: when a revision rewrites the plain sense to state a hard passage plainly, it must still give context from around the verse and not retell the verse, and it must keep Griffith’s wording (his “invocation”) rather than the commentators’ gloss of it (their “callers”); the four word check does not catch a paraphrase that changes every word. The second revision of day 110 shows the form that works: name the verses on either side and what they do. Also from day 110: when a second commentator is added to a source line, each half still needs the name, the work and the place, and the entry check does not test that the work is named, so it must be read by eye.
